- [[books/the-mom-test|The Mom Test]] / `chapter-03-the-mom-test` / `chapter-03-the-mom-test-chunk-001`
  - The chapter introduces 'The Mom Test,' a set of rules for asking good questions that reveal truth about business ideas. It contrasts two conversations with mom about a digital cookbook app for iPad: one that fails by asking leading hypothetical questions that invite false positives, and one that succeeds by asking specific questions about actual behaviors and past examples. The key insight is that
  - You shouldn't ask anyone whether your business is a good idea, at least not in those words
  - Asking leading questions invites people to lie or give false positives
  - It's our responsibility to find the truth by asking good questions
- [[books/the-mom-test|The Mom Test]] / `chapter-03-the-mom-test` / `chapter-03-the-mom-test-chunk-002`
  - Introduction to The Mom Test, a framework for conducting better customer conversations by talking about customers' lives rather than ideas, asking about specifics in the past rather than hypotheticals about the future, and listening more than talking. The chunk provides examples of good and bad customer interview questions.
  - The measure of usefulness of an early customer conversation is whether it gives concrete facts about customers' lives and worldviews
  - Avoid mentioning your idea too soon in customer conversations
  - By avoiding mentioning your idea, you automatically ask better questions
- [[books/the-mom-test|The Mom Test]] / `chapter-04-avoiding-bad-data` / `chapter-04-avoiding-bad-data-chunk-001`
  - This chapter explains how to identify and avoid bad data in customer conversations. Bad data comes in three forms: compliments, fluff, and ideas. The author provides detailed examples of how compliments are misleading and demonstrates techniques to deflect them and redirect conversations toward facts and commitments rather than false validation.
  - Bad data gives false negatives (thinking idea is dead when it's not) and false positives (convincing yourself you're right when you're not)
  - Compliments are almost certainly lying and are worthless as data
  - Opinions are worthless except from industry experts who have built similar businesses

  - The chapter teaches how to identify and ask the most important questions in customer conversations. It demonstrates a bad conversation about a fitness app followed by improved versions that first establish whether the person actually cares about the problem before drilling down into specifics.
  - Premature zooming leads to false positives and worthless data that seems like validation
  - Before zooming in on a specific problem, verify that it's actually a top priority for the customer
  - Generic questions reveal what customers truly care about versus what they'll answer hypothetically
