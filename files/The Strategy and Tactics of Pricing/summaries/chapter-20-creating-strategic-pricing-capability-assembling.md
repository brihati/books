# Chapter 11: Creating Strategic Pricing Capability—Assembling Talent, Processes, and Data to Build Competitive Advantage

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Strategic Pricing Imperative

Organizations struggle to implement value-based pricing despite its proven profitability advantage. A Deloitte benchmarking study of 200+ companies identified four pricing archetypes and found that "Value Masters"—firms employing value-based pricing with strong execution—outperform industry peers by 24% on average operating profitability. Yet more than 60% of sales and marketing managers are frustrated by their organization's ability to improve pricing performance, and more than 75% are unsure what to do to drive effective execution of pricing strategy.

The core challenge is not strategy alone but organizational execution. Good pricing strategy fails when organizational processes and incentives are inconsistent with strategy objectives. Only 9% of business schools offer standalone pricing courses, and only 6% of Fortune 500 companies have a dedicated pricing function, reflecting the widespread lack of pricing skills and organizational capability.

### Four Pricing Archetypes

The Deloitte study identified four organizational types:

- **Value Masters**: Firms employing value-based pricing and excelling at execution
- **Well Intentioned**: Firms with value-based pricing but poor execution
- **Directionally Challenged**: Firms without value-based pricing strategy and weak execution
- **Runaway Trains**: Firms with excellent execution but no value-based pricing strategy

### Root Causes of Pricing Failure

Organizations face three foundational challenges:

1. **Lack of pricing skills**: Insufficient training and dedicated pricing functions
2. **Misaligned incentive structures**: Compensation plans across functions reward behaviors that undermine pricing strategy
3. **Insufficient access to information and tools**: Lack of data and analytical capabilities to inform decisions

**Functional misalignment examples:**
- Sales representatives compensated on revenue alone resist price increases that might lose deals
- Marketers incentivized on market share growth use price promotions that erode brand equity and margins
- Finance executives evaluated on margin percentage argue against low-margin/high-volume opportunities
- Operations executives focus on capacity utilization even when it drives down market prices

The apparel industry illustrates this vulnerability: a 15-year period of declining labor and cotton costs from 1995 reduced pricing urgency. When cost shocks hit from 2008–2012, the industry lacked pricing capability to respond effectively.

### Three Foundational Investments

Value Masters succeed through three underlying investments:

1. **Human talent**: Pricing skills and dedicated personnel
2. **Organizational structure**: Clear roles and decision rights
3. **Data and analytical tools**: Systems to inform decisions and monitor implementation

Imbalanced investment in these components can lead to catastrophe. The airline industry over-invested in data and analytics (yield management tools in the 1980s) while under-investing in the ability to innovate profitable value sources. This led to extensive price wars that in 1991 wiped out cumulative profits earned by airlines up to that point in their history.

### Assessing Pricing Organization Maturity

A simple diagnostic: ask the top five executives to articulate the firm's pricing strategy. If answers differ significantly, the organization likely has a pricing capability maturity problem. Additional assessment criteria include:

- Number of deviations from agreed-upon price schedules
- Clarity about lines of authority for pricing decisions
- Ability to implement upcharges for non-standard requests
- Uncollected charges and write-offs
- Unearned discounts
- Pricing errors
- Order processing and fulfillment errors

### Challenging Industry Norms and Experimentation

High-performing organizations challenge industry norms and experiment with new pricing models. The music industry exemplifies disruption: Apple launched iTunes in 2001 to allow sale of single songs instead of albums; by 2011 digital outpaced physical sales; by 2016 streaming became the dominant revenue model. Kodak invented the original digital camera in 1975 but dismissed it, viewing themselves as in the paper and chemicals business. Uber and Airbnb reimagined transportation and hospitality by not being bound by industry norms. Amazon continuously tried new business models from online bookstore to e-readers to online department store to food delivery to physical bookstores in 2016.

**The IT distributor case**: A company initially offered high-quality services including on-demand technical consulting and next-day delivery to all customers. After losing 20% of revenue to low-cost competitors, it implemented a tiered services model allowing customers to choose lower prices with lower service levels or pay premium for high service levels. Executives initially claimed "everyone needs the high level of service, it's just that some customers don't know it yet." Only after losing 20% of revenue did they test the low-cost offering, which resulted in staggering results—regaining price-sensitive customers profitably through standardized buying processes.

### Netflix Case Study: The Power of Implementation

Netflix's contrasting price increase experiences illustrate how organizational alignment and implementation approach determine success:

**2011 price increase (failure)**: Netflix raised prices up to 60% and split DVD and streaming businesses. This resulted in over 10,000 negative blog posts, 1 million cancelled subscriptions, and loss of more than half market value within two months. The CEO issued a public apology.

**2014 price increase (success)**: Netflix implemented a 12.5% increase on May 12, 2014, and gained 1.7 million new subscribers during the quarter, with estimated $500 million incremental revenue by 2017. Success came from segmenting customers and only applying the increase to new subscribers while providing price guidance of $1–$2 range. The key difference: how the price increase was communicated and implemented significantly affected customer response.

### Organizational Structure for Pricing

Three archetypal pricing structures exist:

**Centralized Pricing**: Pricing decisions made and managed at the corporate level. Effective for single markets or similar business contexts. Common in commoditized industries like energy and airlines where cost and yield management drive success. Major airlines manage pricing centrally due to complexity of multiple routes and cascading demand effects.

**Center of Expertise**: Business units maintain control of pricing decisions while central group shares best practices and provides specialized skills like advanced analytics. Appropriate when business units operate in markets with significant variations in competitive pressures, product specifications, or customer buying patterns.

**Dedicated Support Unit**: Each business unit has a dedicated pricing group loosely aligned with corporate pricing. Appropriate for diversified businesses with little market overlap.

**The bank case**: A bank's country managers, assessed on volume and share targets, customized discount programs unsupported by billing systems, creating hundreds of untracked discounts and millions in uninvoiced services. The company resolved this by creating weekly contract review meetings with key decision-makers to ensure consistency while allowing local market reflection. A major airline offered 18 different prices for flights from New York to Miami across more than 325 routes.

### Decision Rights Framework

Formal allocation of pricing decision rights prevents inconsistent pricing and managerial conflict. Decision rights define the scope and role of each person's participation in the decision-making process:

- **Input rights**: Enable an individual to be an accepted source of specified information necessary for the decision
- **Make rights**: Should belong to only one person or committee to ensure clear accountability
- **Ratify rights**: Provide a mechanism for senior managers to overturn pricing decisions when they conflict with broader organizational priorities
- **Notify rights**: Allocated to individuals that will use or be affected by pricing decisions in other decision-making processes

A business services company created a key account team without coordinating with the traditional sales force, resulting in large accounts receiving competing and inconsistent price quotes, which led customers to solicit additional quotes and caused average selling prices to decline rapidly despite account growth.

### Pricing Processes and Profit Leaks

Strategic pricing spans all activities that contribute to profitable commercial outcomes, not just price-setting. Pricing processes should be defined broadly to include negotiation, contracting, and decisions about ancillary charges.

**Profit leaks** are losses in profitability caused by unwarranted or unmanaged discounts, incurrence of unnecessary costs, or unmet terms and conditions. More than 58% of managers indicated their incentive plans likely encourage choices that reduce company profits.

### Sales Compensation Alignment

Revenue-based commissions create perverse incentives that prioritize volume over profitability. Sales representatives on revenue-based commissions will prioritize closing multiple low-margin deals over investing time in high-margin deals, even when both generate equal profit contribution.

**Example**: A sales rep investing twice as much time to increase profit by 10% on a deal earns only 10% more commission, while a colleague closing two lower-margin deals earns twice the commission.

**Solution**: Link compensation to profitability using a profitability factor (or "kicker"):

**Sales Credit = [Target Price – k(Target Price – Actual Price)] × Units Sold**

Where k = 1 divided by the product's percentage contribution margin at the target price.

**Example with 20% contribution margin**: The profitability factor equals 5. When a salesperson grants a 15% price discount, the sales credit is reduced by 75% rather than 15%. A $1,000 product sold for $850 produces only $250 of sales credit, while a $500 product sold for $550 (10% price premium) earns $750 of sales credit.

**The high-tech manufacturer case**: A finance group enforced a 64% minimum gross margin policy, causing the sales organization (measured on volume) to spend hours devising workarounds to get approval for high-volume, lower-margin deals.

When salespeople transition to value selling and profit-based compensation, those who previously complained about high prices begin complaining about slow deliveries, quality defects, lack of innovative product features, and need for better sales support.

### Pricing Analytics and Data Systems

Systems capability for pricing requires three components:

1. **Objective data**: Win-loss data, historical transaction data, competitive pricing information
2. **Analytics protocols**: Win-loss analysis, customer profitability analysis, price banding, price waterfall analysis
3. **Tools**: Commercial-grade pricing software with alerts for discount thresholds and historical price-trend data

**Win-loss data** is probably the most valuable information a company can collect for pricing strategy. Win-loss ratios should be compared across time periods and similar geographical areas or end-use applications. The ultimate goal is not to maximize wins but to maximize profit contribution earned.

**Customer profitability analysis** requires assessing prices paid by specific customers combined with cost-to-serve measures. Most accounting systems do not allocate costs at the customer level or do so arbitrarily. Customer profitability mapping charts customers based on average selling price and cost to serve:

- **Platinum customers** (high price, low cost-to-serve): Protect by understanding why they pay premium prices and ensuring good value
- **Lead/outlaw customers** (low price, high cost-to-serve): Address by reducing cost-to-serve or raising prices

**Financial services firm example**: Used customer profitability analysis charting each customer based on average selling price and cost to serve. Individual customer profitability analysis helped management implement increased automation and bulking of claims, reducing direct labor and processing costs and driving 37% improvement in profitability.

### Price Banding and Price Waterfall Analysis

**Price banding** is a statistical technique for identifying which customers are paying significantly more or significantly less than the band of peer prices for a given type of transaction. Price band analysis reveals whether pricing variation is justified by legitimate factors or is random and damaging.

**Price waterfall analysis** tracks multiple sources of profit leakage beyond invoice price discounts. **Pocket price**—revenue actually earned after all discounts are netted out—is often much less than invoice price.

**Example**: A company discovered sales to some customers resulted in more leakages than the gross margin at list price, with multiple layers of discounts: salesperson commission, volume incentive for retailer, commission for buying group, co-op advertising incentive, inventory holding incentive, early payment discount, end customer coupon, and coupon processing fees.

**Large distributor case**: Imposed documentation requirements for waived shipping charges, resulting in tens of millions of dollars added to the bottom line.

### Pricing Systems and Strategy Execution

Pricing strategy should dictate systems requirements, not the other way around. System design choices can enhance or hinder a company's ability to execute pricing strategy. Too many companies adopt pricing technology that is seldom used or eventually abandoned.

Four distinct functionalities exist in commercial pricing systems:

1. **Pricing analytics**: Ability to create price waterfalls, price bands, segmentation analytics, and dashboards to monitor pricing trends
2. **Price optimization**: Execution of statistical models to calculate price points optimizing against objectives such as profit, unit volume, or market share
3. **Price management**: Tools allowing users to set rules and conditions that automatically change prices in dynamic fashion, managing price lists by market, customer, and product category
4. **Deal management**: Functionality that pulls together data to enable salespeople to negotiate more profitable quotes and contracts while ensuring deals remain within pricing and discount policy bounds

**McDonald's example**: A manager unable to apply percentage discounts because cash registers had pre-programmed prices for specific menu items. The manager successfully reframed the request as 24 Happy Meals for the price of 22.

**Airlines example**: Captured over $38 billion in non-ticket revenues in 2014 through systems enabling new pricing strategies for checked bags, window seats, in-flight meals, and Wi-Fi access.

### Organizational Change Management

Transforming an organization to execute strategic pricing requires that managers act in ways that may run counter to their past experience and training. Organizational culture change is necessary to support systems investment.

**Readiness assessment questions**:
- What would the organization like to achieve?
- What are key benefits and are they aligned with corporate strategy?
- Has senior leadership bought in?
- Who will be affected and is there a plan for stakeholder success?
- What is the appetite to absorb change?

**Senior management leadership**: Senior managers must demonstrate commitment to new pricing strategies through consistent actions, not just verbal support. Ad hoc discounting by senior leaders undermines new pricing strategies and reverts organizations to old practices.

**Telecommunications company case**: A company invested in strategic pricing for consumer markets but the COO undermined the strategy by offering "one-time" discounts to meet sales targets, causing regional sales managers to demand similar discounts and the strategy to be abandoned.

**Demonstration projects**: Focused, limited-duration tests of new pricing approaches provide examples of "what good looks like" and build momentum for organizational adoption. Projects should use control groups to provide objective evidence of strategy effectiveness.

**Entertainment company example**: Tested a new pricing strategy during a specific post-holiday period with one focused metric to track gross profit growth, using a control sample of similar products to establish a baseline.
