# Chapter 4: Price Structure Tactics for Pricing Differently Across Customer Segments

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### Foundation: Why Segmented Pricing Matters

A well-designed segmented price structure allows companies to capture substantially more revenue by charging different prices to different customer segments based on their willingness to pay and cost to serve. Individual customers value products differently due to differences in ability to pay, subjective preferences, end-use applications, and prior experience. A single-price strategy forces trade-offs between volume and margin, leaving excess money on the table from buyers willing to pay more while excluding nearly half the market that could be served profitably.

For industries with high fixed costs, serving additional customers at lower prices is often essential for survival. Companies with large market share that refuse to serve lower-value segments risk losing market position to competitors who address that need—a pattern documented extensively in Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma*. The railroad industry exemplifies this principle: coal and unprocessed grains are carried at much lower cost per carload than manufactured goods, but large volumes enable substantial contribution to fixed costs. Xerox's loss of dominant position in the high-end copier market occurred after companies entering at the bottom developed service networks large enough to support sales of higher-priced copiers.

### Three Mechanisms for Segmented Pricing

Companies can create segmented price structures through three mechanisms: **offer configurations**, **price metrics**, and **price fences**.

#### Offer Configurations and Bundling

Offer configurations enable self-selection, minimizing enforcement needs. Bundled pricing reduces transaction costs and makes customers less sensitive to individual feature costs. Research shows convincingly that people are less sensitive to the cost of value-added features and services when bundled as a single expenditure.

Bundling is most effective when different customer segments rank the importance of features differently. Airlines bundle seat changes with higher-priced fares for business travelers while offering lower fares to tour operators bundled with hotel and attraction tickets. Cable TV operators create different bundles for families, sports enthusiasts, and movie buffs based on programming preferences. A sports channel bundle of fight sports and team sports at $39/month generates more revenue than separate prices of $18/month and $33/month, producing $8/month additional revenue per subscriber.

**Selective uglification**—adding features to lower-cost offers that reduce value for higher-priced segments without affecting discount segment value—allows segment-specific pricing without explicit price cuts. Resort hotels bundle kids' clubs free for one child with each paying adult, free breakfast buffet admission for children, and shuttle/discount tickets to family entertainment. Chemical companies add substances to industrial-grade chemicals to make them unacceptable for food manufacturers.

**Unbundling** is strategically essential in competitive markets to avoid margin erosion from high-cost-to-serve customers. Airlines unbundle baggage handling and agent reservation assistance as per-use fees. Unless the cost to deliver a service is trivial relative to overall value, bundling optional services for free undermines profitability.

#### Price Metrics

Price metrics define the units to which price is applied and the terms of exchange—what exactly the buyer receives per unit of price paid. Better price metrics than competitors provide margin gains from existing customers and incremental revenue from previously priced-out markets.

**Value-based price metrics** allow cost-effective providers to compete without convincing payers to pay more per service. Kaiser Permanente and Mayo Clinic adopted capitation pricing or per-illness pricing. Eli Lilly priced Prozac per day of therapy rather than per milligram, charging the same per pill regardless of dosage. Software companies evolved from "price per server" to "price per seat" to "price per production unit" as they discovered better metrics.

Five criteria determine profitable price metrics:

1. **Objective measurement**: The metric must be objectively measured or verified to avoid conflicts and misinformation. When price depends on customers voluntarily reporting information that leads to higher prices, potential for conflicts is almost always too high.

2. **Tracking with value and cost**: The metric should track with both customer value and company cost differences across segments.

3. **Competitive positioning**: The metric should make pricing appear attractive compared to competitors. A lubricant company enabled manufacturers to cut through materials faster; it used published industrial sales index to adjust prices monthly based on customer capacity utilization during recession. Hosted voice-recognition software changed from cost-per-minute (72% premium) to cost-per-call (5% premium), making the offer more competitive.

4. **Alignment with buyer experience**: The metric should align with how buyers experience value in use. Netflix changed from Blockbuster's daily charge metric ($3.95) to monthly membership fee ($8.99 for one DVD, $13.99 for two), eliminating inconvenience and aligning with value experienced.

5. **Regulatory compliance**: In regulated markets, ensure new price metrics comply with regulatory and legal requirements.

**Performance-based pricing** ties customer payment directly to economic value received, shifting performance risk from buyer to seller. GE's GE90 aircraft engines faced buyer resistance due to uncertainty about maintenance costs. GE changed the price metric from selling/leasing engines alone to renting them per hour flown with all maintenance included, making them quickly popular despite price premium. Internet ads are priced based on click-throughs rather than cost per thousand exposures. Office building lighting and cooling systems are installed in return for contracts sharing energy cost savings.

#### Freemium Models and In-App Purchases

Smartphone adoption created a massive new gaming market with 2.9 billion users, compared to 529 million game consoles sold 2008-2016. The traditional price-per-title metric for video games did not allow for differences in value across player segments or track cost to serve. The freemium model—free download with in-app purchases—better aligns revenue with player engagement.

In-app purchases accounted for 79% of iOS mobile app revenue at the start of 2014 (94% in Asian markets). Games with in-app purchases earn up to three times more revenue per game than upfront purchase models. Three types of in-app purchases exist:

- **Consumables**: help players continue playing or ascend levels, used once then consumed (virtual poker chips in Zynga Poker)
- **Durables**: help players succeed but are not consumed (improved billiard cue in 8 Ball Pool)
- **Personalization**: customize user experience without directly impacting gameplay (clothes for game avatar in Highrise)

#### Price Fences

Price fences are fixed criteria that customers must meet to qualify for a lower price. They are the least complicated way to charge different prices to reflect different levels of value when a good proxy metric does not correlate with value differences.

**Buyer identification fences** rely on observable customer characteristics. Theaters and museums use age-based price fences (discounts for children under 12 and seniors) and educational status (full-time student discounts). Barbers charge different prices for short and long hair because long hair takes more time to cut, and offer substantial discounts for children's haircuts during non-peak hours to drive business with price-sensitive segments.

**Self-identification methods** induce price-sensitive customers to volunteer identification information. AARP cards and Medicare cards are used for senior discounts at hotels, rental car companies, theaters, and restaurants. Coupons provided by sellers give deal-prone shoppers a way to identify themselves; supermarkets and drug stores put coupons in advertising circulars because people who read those ads are part of the segment that compares prices before deciding where to shop.

**Purchase location fences** enable segmentation when customers perceiving different values buy at different locations. Colorado ski resorts charge slope-side tickets at highest price, Dillon hotel tickets approximately 10% less, and Denver grocery store tickets approximately 20% less. Freight absorption allows sellers to segment buyers by location without cutting prices uniformly: a steel mill in Pittsburgh charged based on shipping cost from Pittsburgh or Gary, Indiana.

Before EU membership, German luxury cars sold in Britain were at least 20% more expensive than in Belgium. German luxury car makers used warranty transfer charges to enforce location fences in the UK to prevent cross-border arbitrage.

**Time-of-purchase fences** work when different customer segments purchase at different times. Theaters offer midday matinees at reduced prices for price-sensitive viewers. Restaurants charge more for evening patrons due to fewer inexpensive dinner substitutes. Furniture manufacturers conduct sales in February and August when most people would not normally think about buying furniture.

### Quantity-Based Pricing Tactics

**Order discounts** encourage large, infrequent orders by reducing per-unit processing and shipping costs. Many order processing costs are unrelated to order size, so per-unit costs decline with quantity ordered. Copier paper costs about $20 per case of ten reams but several dollars per individual ream.

**Volume discounts** are based on customer's total purchases over a month or year rather than amount purchased at one time. Steel manufacturers grant auto companies substantially lower prices than other industrial buyers due to large volumes. Customers who buy in large volume are usually more price-sensitive because they have greater financial incentive to negotiate and learn about alternatives. Costs of selling and servicing an account generally do not increase proportionately with purchase volume.

**Step discounts** apply only to purchases beyond a specified amount, not to total quantity. An electric company charges $0.06 per KWH for general electricity usage but $0.04 per KWH for heating, implemented as a step-price schedule: $0.06 per KWH for first 100 KWH block and $0.04 thereafter. Step discounting may segment not only different customers, but also different purchases by the same customers.

**Two-part pricing** addresses situations with high incremental costs for new customers but lower costs for additional volume. Fuel oil or LPG suppliers charge a price for delivery plus a price per unit of product.

### Peak Pricing and Yield Management

**Peak pricing** raises prices reactively whenever supply is inadequate to meet quantity demanded. Uber adjusts prices upward when demand exceeds supply. London and Singapore charge congestion charges for inner-city driving. U.S. highways with priority lanes use access charges to manage capacity. Peak pricing can both improve quality of life while earning revenues disproportionately from people willing to pay premiums at peak times.

**Yield management** is a sophisticated pricing technique that uses segmented pricing, demand forecasting, and price elasticity estimation to optimize revenue and profit by targeting price changes to price-sensitive customers while maintaining pricing for less price-sensitive segments. Three tasks are required: segmented pricing based on value, demand forecasting by segment over time, and estimating demand price elasticity by segment.

Airlines pioneered yield management as a huge competitive advantage. On a Boston to Chicago route, a fully refundable, changeable fare for business travelers is $302 for all flights during work week. Monday morning peak demand shows non-refundable, non-changeable tickets at popular flight times at $232 to discourage discretionary travelers. Wednesday lower demand shows non-refundable fares reduced to $127, which is 37% below Monday price, allowing airlines to sell greater than 37% more seats. The key to transforming simple peak pricing into sophisticated yield management is forecasting demand and adjusting price based on how current sales track against forecasted sales.

Yield management software packages are now widely used for airline seats, hotel rooms, rental cars, advertising space, and concert tickets.

### Historical Context: The Sound Dues Reform

A 15th-century example illustrates value-based pricing principles. Erik of Pomerania, King of the United Kingdom of Scandinavia, imposed a toll of "one English noble" for ships passing Elsinore. Subsequent kings raised this to three nobles. When Frederik II acceded to the throne in 1559 at age 25, his seven-year war with Sweden drained Denmark's finances. Peder Oxe, enlisted in 1566 to reform Denmark's finances, radically changed the Sound Dues system from a simple per-ship toll to a cargo-value-based percentage system of two Rixdollars per last (approximately two tons). The King's right of pre-emption encouraged accurate cargo declarations while creating incentive dilemmas for ship captains—an early example of using price structure to align incentives.

## Key Points

- Individual customers value products differently; a single-price strategy forces volume-margin trade-offs
- Segmented pricing can substantially mitigate this trade-off and serve additional market segments profitably
- Three mechanisms enable segmented pricing: offer configurations, price metrics, and price fences
- Customers have incentive to undermine segmented pricing by disguising themselves as lower-price-qualified buyers
- Channel intermediaries can undermine segmented pricing through gray-market sales
- Bundling features valued differently by segments maximizes revenue when incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost
- Selective uglification adds features to lower-priced offers that reduce value for higher-priced segments
- Unbundling variable-cost services protects margins in competitive markets
- Value-based price metrics allow cost-effective providers to compete without requiring higher per-service payments
- Price metrics must be objectively measured, track with value and cost, and align with buyer experience
- Performance-based pricing shifts risk from buyer to seller
- Freemium models with in-app purchases align revenue with player engagement in digital products
- Price fences use observable criteria to segment customers without explicit price cuts
- Quantity discounts (order, volume, step) reflect cost differences and segment by purchase behavior
- Peak pricing and yield management optimize revenue by targeting price changes to price-sensitive segments
- Flexible pricing undermines profitability in ongoing commercial relationships

## Definitions and Terms

- **Segmented price structure**: Different prices charged to different customer segments based on willingness to pay and cost to serve
- **Consumer surplus**: Excess value enjoyed by buyers when they pay less than their willingness to pay
- **Gray-market sales**: Channel intermediaries buying products intended for lower-price customers and diverting them for resale to higher-price customers
- **Offer configurations**: Different bundles of features and services configured for different customer segments
- **Price metrics**: Units to which price is applied; they define terms of exchange
- **Price fences**: Fixed criteria that customers must meet to qualify for a lower price
- **Selective uglification**: Adding features to lower-cost offers that reduce value for higher-priced segments without affecting discount segment value
- **Capitation price**: Covers all services required by a patient during a year
- **Value-based price metrics**: Pricing structures earning revenues unrelated to quantity of product or service provided
- **Performance-based pricing**: Price structures where price is based on performance received and incremental cost to serve, shifting performance risk from buyer to seller
- **Freemium model**: Games free for end-user to download, then pay for value delivered within the game
- **Consumables**: In-app purchases that help player continue playing or ascend levels, used once then consumed
- **Durables**: In-app purchases that help player succeed but are not consumed
- **Personalization**: In-app purchases that customize user experience without directly impacting gameplay
- **Buyer identification fences**: Price fences relying on observable customer characteristics
- **Self-identification methods**: Inducing price-sensitive customers to volunteer identification information
