# Chapter 3: Price and Value Communication Strategies to Influence Willingness-to-Pay

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Value Communication Gap

Value communication is rated as the most important capability for pricing strategies, yet remains one of the weakest capabilities in most sales and marketing organizations. The fundamental challenge is that sellers often substitute feature communication for value communication, forcing customers to independently divine potential benefits. Even the most carefully constructed value-based pricing strategy will fail unless the offer's value and differentiation is understood by potential customers. Customers who do not recognize differential value are vulnerable to buying inferior offerings at lower prices.

The role of value and price communications is to convey value propositions to enable customer understanding, improve willingness-to-pay, and increase purchase likelihood. This requires deep understanding of customer value combined with detailed understanding of how and why customers buy.

### Developing Value Communication Strategy

Effective value communication strategies are framed by two key dimensions:

**1. Type of Value Delivered**
- **Economic value**: measurable monetary benefits such as profit, cost savings, or productivity
- **Psychological value**: benefits such as comfort, appearance, pleasure, status, health, or personal fulfillment

**2. Degree of Buyer Involvement**
- **Low-involvement products**: consumers establish preferences through trial rather than extensive research
- **High-involvement products**: products requiring careful personal decision-making and evaluation of competing offers

Virtually every purchase has both economic and psychological elements. Combining financial and psychological value messages can effectively justify price premiums. For products with monetary value drivers, value quantification should be central to messaging to highlight gaps between customer perceptions and actual value. For psychological benefits, value messaging should focus on how a product makes the customer feel.

**Communication Strategies by Product Type:**

For low-involvement products with psychological benefits, value communication should focus on psychological benefits and create offers that make it easier to try the product. Low-involvement products with economic benefits require clear communication of cost savings and longevity benefits on packaging.

For high-involvement products with psychological benefits, celebrity endorsements and customer testimonials are effective. High-involvement products with economic benefits require careful deliberation and evaluation of benefits relative to cost differentials, often using the Economic Value Estimation (EVE®) model as an interactive tool on laptops or tablets to illustrate value propositions with detailed data and assumptions.

### Behavioral Economics and Price Perception

Most customers do not make purchase decisions based purely on economic value analysis. Psychological factors such as prestige, convenience, safety, and fairness often outweigh economic considerations. Behavioral economics shows that buyers depart from economic rationality in systematic and predictable ways, using heuristics and mental shortcuts to guide purchase decisions when fully informed choices are difficult.

**The Competitive-Reference Effect** explains how customers assess value by comparing to a visible competitive reference product. Customers tend to choose options in the middle of an assortment to avoid appearing too cheap or too extravagant. Sellers can influence willingness-to-pay by managing customer understanding of relevant competitive alternatives.

### Price Sensitivity Effects

Three major effects influence buyer price sensitivity:

**1. Switching-Cost Effect**
The greater the product-specific investment that a buyer must make to switch suppliers, the less price sensitive that buyer is when choosing between alternatives. Switching costs can be both monetary (retraining, spare parts investment, qualification processes) and non-monetary (personal relationships, trust). Airlines are reluctant to switch from Boeing to Airbus planes due to retraining costs and spare parts investment. Busy executives are reluctant to switch accountants, lawyers, or childcare providers despite lower-cost alternatives.

**2. Difficult-Comparison Effect**
Buyers are less sensitive to the price of a known or reputable supplier when they have difficulty comparing alternatives. Brand reputation and trust allow suppliers to command price premiums. Industrial buyers are brand loyal and price insensitive when products are difficult to evaluate and failure costs are high.

**3. End-Benefit Effect**
A buyer's price sensitivity is influenced by the importance of the benefit that they are trying to derive from their purchase. Greater risk and higher cost of failure increase salience of this effect.

### The Price-Quality Effect

The price-quality effect states that buyers are positively influenced by higher prices because they may signal better quality. Price serves as a quality signal for image products, exclusive products, and products without other quality cues. Prestige brands command higher prices and reduce price sensitivity based on recognition and ego gratification. High prices can provide exclusivity value by limiting clientele and improving service quality.

Consumers rely on price as a quality cue when they cannot ascertain objective quality before purchase. Price can influence actual product effectiveness, not just perceived quality. In one study, students who paid full price for an energy drink performed better on an exam than those who received a discount.

### Transaction Utility and Fairness

Buyers are motivated by both acquisition utility (the utility from using the product) and transaction utility (the psychological value associated with the difference between the price paid and what the buyer considers a reasonable or fair offer). The reference price—the amount that the buyer would consider reasonable or fair—determines customer satisfaction with the transaction.

**The Fairness Effect** reflects that a "fair price" is a community-held norm not guided by profitability, absolute value, or supply. Fairness norms change over time and vary by industry. The form of price change impacts fairness perception more than economic impact. Elimination of discounts is viewed as fair price increase, but adding surcharges is viewed negatively despite the same economic impact.

Fairness is framed by the "shadow of the future"—long-term relationships enforce stricter fairness norms. One-time transactions allow market rate pricing, but anticipated future interactions enforce stricter fairness norms. During the Dark Ages, merchants faced death penalties for exceeding public norms on "just price." During the Communist period, those charging more than official prices were regarded as criminals. After Hurricane Sandy, the New Jersey governor warned against price gouging despite supply shortages.

**The Shared-Cost Effect** describes how the smaller the portion of price buyers pay themselves, the less price sensitive they are. Cost-sharing through tax deductions and insurance reduces price sensitivity to beneficial behaviors. Higher-income buyers accept higher prices as a substitute for time spent shopping.

**The Expenditure Effect** indicates that buyers are more price sensitive when the expenditure is larger, either in dollar terms or as a percentage of available budget.

### Multiple Stakeholders in B2B Purchasing

Multiple stakeholders in B2B purchases value different aspects of an offer and require tailored messages. Aggregate value estimates are not equally relevant to different stakeholders in customer organizations. Different functional areas within a customer organization experience different value impacts from the same product.

In a chemical additive example for a steel mini-mill with $18 per ton aggregate value, the melt shop foreman values $2 per ton from reduced scrap rate but dislikes $5 per ton cost from additional process steps. The finish mill supervisor may be negatively disposed toward the product despite overall positive value because it lowers his organization's financial performance. The procurement agent has neutral value impact as her functional area has no operational involvement with the additive.

Purchasing agents in B2B settings are incentivized to choose the lowest-priced offer because it requires less justification and effort. Sellers must provide purchasing agents with documented economic benefits and justification for price premiums to overcome this incentive.

### Reframing and Positioning

Market demand is not fixed; value communication can differentiate offerings and reframe price comparisons even in commoditized markets. Woolite laundry detergent reframed its product as an alternative to dry cleaning rather than competing against conventional detergents, allowing it to defend a significant price premium.

Decoy pricing strategies can shift customer perception of appropriate price points. A business machine company introduced a fourth, even more expensive model to its product line; the new model sold poorly but sales of the previously top-end model increased dramatically. Restaurant wine lists include expensive bottles like $3,700 2010 Chateau Petrus that are rarely purchased and sometimes not even in the cellar, serving as decoys to influence perception of other wines.

## Key Points

1. Value communication is the most important yet weakest capability in most sales and marketing organizations
2. Sellers often communicate features rather than benefits, forcing customers to determine value independently
3. Effective value communication requires understanding both the type of value (economic vs. psychological) and degree of buyer involvement (low vs. high)
4. Behavioral economics shows buyers use heuristics and mental shortcuts rather than pure economic rationality
5. The Competitive-Reference Effect allows sellers to influence willingness-to-pay by managing customer understanding of alternatives
6. Switching costs, difficult comparisons, and end-benefits significantly reduce price sensitivity
7. The price-quality effect means higher prices can signal better quality and increase willingness-to-pay
8. Transaction utility (fairness perception) matters as much as acquisition utility (product use)
9. Fairness norms are community-held standards that vary by industry and change over time
10. Multiple stakeholders in B2B purchases require different, tailored value messages
11. Value communication can differentiate offerings even in commoditized markets
12. Decoy pricing and reframing can shift customer perception of appropriate price points

## Important Examples and Stories

**Feature vs. Benefit Communication:**
- Mobile phones advertised with "64GB memory" or "4G connectivity" without translating these features into customer benefits such as ability to store a specific number of pictures or improvements in reception quality

**Effective Value Reframing:**
- Michelin tire advertisement featuring a plump baby sitting in a tire with the message "Remember what is riding on your tires" reframes automotive tires as important safety devices rather than commodities
- Woolite laundry detergent reframed its product as an alternative to dry cleaning rather than competing against conventional detergents, allowing it to defend a significant price premium

**Medical Device Pricing:**
- Drug-coated coronary stent priced at $3,500 versus $1,750 for uncoated stents. Stent implantation costs $30,000+; uncoated stents reclogs in 20% of cases requiring repeat $30,000 procedure; drug-eluting stent reduces reclogging to 5%, creating $4,500 objective differentiation value plus psychological benefit of avoiding repeat procedure risk and discomfort

**Decoy Pricing:**
- A business machine company introduced a fourth, even more expensive model to its product line; the new model sold poorly but sales of the previously top-end model increased dramatically
- Restaurant wine lists include expensive bottles like $3,700 2010 Chateau Petrus that are rarely purchased and sometimes not even in the cellar, serving as decoys to influence perception of other wines

**Switching Costs:**
- Food ingredient coming off patent: buyers demanded price reductions to match generic suppliers, but switching costs (qualification process of 3-6 months, factory audits, pilot runs, supply chain redesign, local warehouse requirements) made switching uneconomical

**Price-Quality Effect:**
- Energy drink experiment: students who paid full price performed better on exam than those who received discount

**Fairness Norms:**
- Car negotiation scenario where dealer's immediate acceptance of opening offer versus counteroffer creates different transaction utilities despite lower final price
- Restaurateurs in resort areas charge higher prices than local restaurants because transient tourists are unaware of better alternatives and rely on signals like convenient locations and good signage rather than price comparison

**Multiple Stakeholders:**
- Chemical company selling a new chemical additive for a steel mini-mill with $18 per ton aggregate value distributed unevenly across stakeholders with different perspectives

## Definitions and Terms

- **Feature communication**: sellers point to extensive product specifications in hopes that buyers recognize where and how these features deliver benefits
- **Value communication**: conveying value propositions in a compelling manner to enable customers to understand benefits, improve willingness-to-pay, and increase purchase likelihood
- **Economic value**: measurable monetary benefits such as profit, cost savings, or productivity
- **Psychological value**: benefits such as comfort, appearance, pleasure, status, health, or personal fulfillment
- **Low-involvement products**: products where consumers establish preferences through trial rather than extensive research
- **High-involvement products**: products requiring careful personal decision-making and evaluation of competing offers
- **Competitive-Reference Effect**: using a highly visible and high-cost brand in the market as a mental shortcut to assess relative value
- **Switching-Cost Effect**: the greater the product-specific investment that a buyer must make to switch suppliers, the less price sensitive that buyer is when choosing between alternatives
- **Difficult-Comparison Effect**: buyers are less sensitive to the price of a known or reputable supplier when they have difficulty comparing alternatives
- **End-Benefit Effect**: a buyer's price sensitivity is influenced by the importance of the benefit that they are trying to derive from their purchase
- **Price-quality effect**: buyers are positively influenced by a higher price, because it may signal better quality
- **Transaction utility**: the psychological value associated with the difference between the price paid and what the buyer considers a reasonable or fair offer
- **Reference price**: the amount that the buyer would consider reasonable or fair
- **Acquisition utility**: the utility associated with obtaining and using a product
- **Fairness Effect**: a "fair price" is a community-held norm affecting pricing acceptance
- **Shared-Cost Effect**: the effect of partial or complete reimbursement on price sensitivity; smaller the portion of purchase price buyers must pay themselves, the less price sensitive they are
- **Expenditure Effect**: buyers are more price sensitive when the expenditure is larger, either in dollar terms or as a percentage of available budget
- **Economic Value Estimation (EVE®) model**: a value estimation model used to develop customer-specific estimates of product value

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Translate features into benefits**: Translate product features into specific customer benefits rather than listing specifications. Help customers recognize linkages between product's differentiated features and salient value drivers.

2. **Overcome procurement incentives**: Provide purchasing agents with documented economic benefits and justification for price premiums to overcome the incentive to choose lowest-priced offers.

3. **Segment value messages by stakeholder**: Break down value stories into discrete messages and deliver the most relevant story to each stakeholder in the buying process. Adapt value communications to different stakeholders in the customer organization based on their specific functional areas and value priorities.

4. **Differentiate in commoditized markets**: Use value communication to differentiate offerings and reframe price comparisons even in commoditized markets.

5. **Leverage behavioral economics**: Leverage quantifiable benefits and behavioral economics
