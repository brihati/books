# Chapter 9: Financial Analysis—Analyzing Costs and Profits for Pricing

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter addresses how to integrate internal financial considerations with external market considerations in pricing decisions. The core principle is that **only incremental, avoidable costs matter for pricing**—not average costs, sunk costs, or non-incremental fixed costs.

### Foundational Concepts

The chapter distinguishes between antagonistic forces in pricing: financial managers who set prices high to achieve profit objectives, and marketing/sales staff who analyze buyers to set prices low for sales objectives. Neither approach optimizes firm profitability. Instead, effective pricing requires making trade-offs between both internal financial and external market constraints.

A critical insight is that **margin goals, return on capital goals, and operating profit goals are irrelevant to pricing decisions once an investment is made**. These goals matter only when deciding whether to invest in entering a market or offering products. Once committed, the best choice between alternative price points is determined entirely by which generates the most **profit contribution**—revenue minus incremental, avoidable costs.

### Cost Classification for Pricing

The chapter explains three cost categories:

1. **Variable costs** (costs of doing business): Always incremental for pricing decisions because they change with sales volume. Examples include raw materials and direct labor.

2. **Fixed costs** (costs of being in business): Generally not relevant for pricing unless they directly result from implementing a price change. Examples include product design, advertising, and overhead.

3. **Semifixed costs**: Fixed over a range of sales but vary when sales go outside that range. These must be evaluated to determine if they are incremental for a particular pricing decision.

**Incremental costs** are those associated with changes in pricing and sales that rise or fall in total when prices change. **Avoidable costs** are those that either have not yet been incurred or can be reversed, including selling costs, delivery costs, inventory replacement, and rental costs not covered by long-term leases. **Sunk costs**—costs a company is irreversibly committed to bear—should never influence pricing decisions.

### Practical Examples of Cost Relevance

A manufacturer filling orders for up to 100 additional units monthly without purchasing new equipment has non-incremental equipment costs. If orders increased by 150 units monthly requiring new equipment, the added equipment cost becomes incremental. Similarly, a restaurant printing menus with new prices incurs an incremental fixed cost when deciding whether to make price changes, as does an airline advertising a new discount service.

The chapter demonstrates through a music festival case study that **prices below fully allocated average cost can be profitable** when much of the cost is not incremental to additional sales. The town business manager increased ticket prices from $90 to $100, causing attendance to drop from 1,000 to 914 tickets, generating $7,260 profit but hurting civic pride. Analysis showed that much of the festival's $15,000 planning overhead and $40,000 performer compensation was not incremental to additional sales.

### Replacement Cost vs. Historical Cost

A critical distinction is between **replacement cost** (NIFO—next-in, first-out accounting) and **historical cost** (LIFO accounting). When crude oil prices rise, oil companies must raise gasoline prices immediately to cover the cost of replacing inventory, even though existing inventory was purchased at lower prices. The relevant cost for pricing is the future replacement cost of crude oil, not the historical cost of oil already purchased.

### Breakeven Sales Analysis

The chapter introduces a three-step procedure for evaluating price changes:

1. **Define a baseline**: Current profitability or hypothetical scenario
2. **Calculate breakeven sales response**: Determine the minimum sales increase needed for a price reduction to maintain profitability, or maximum sales decline a price increase can tolerate
3. **Assess achievability**: Determine whether that response is realistic given market conditions

The **Westside Manufacturing** case illustrates this approach. Westside makes high-quality pillows with baseline sales of 4,000 units at $10.00 per unit wholesale price, generating $40,000 revenue with $5.50 per unit variable costs ($22,000 total) and $15,000 fixed costs, resulting in $18,000 contribution. Considering a 5% price cut to $9.50 per unit:

- **Price effect**: Loss of $2,000 in contribution on the 4,000 units that could have been sold at the higher price ($0.50 × 4,000)
- **Volume effect**: Contribution earned from increased sales at the new $4.00 contribution margin per unit
- **Breakeven**: 500 additional units (12.5% sales increase) needed to offset the price effect

The formula is: **Breakeven sales change = (−ΔPrice) / (Contribution margin + ΔPrice)**

For Westside: (−$0.50) / ($4.50 − $0.50) = 12.5%

### Dynamic Pricing and Capacity Constraints

The **Ritter & Sons** case study demonstrates how relevant costs vary by circumstances. Ritter, a wholesale producer of potted plants, examined pricing for potted chrysanthemums with:

- **Labor costs**: $7,000 fixed and $44,850 ($0.52 per unit) variable
- **Greenhouse capacity**: 5,000 mums per crop, three crops per year (45,000 annual capacity)
- **Annual greenhouse cost**: $9,000 (depreciation, interest, maintenance, heating)

The critical insight: **Capital costs are incremental only when capacity is fully utilized**. Greenhouses operate at full capacity in only one of three growing seasons. Therefore:

- **Peak season**: $1.80 per mum capital cost (incremental—additional capacity must be built)
- **Off-peak seasons**: $0 capital cost (non-incremental—excess capacity available)

This led to recommendations for **dynamic pricing**: lower prices in off-peak seasons ($3.65, a 5% cut) for large buyers, and higher prices in peak seasons ($4.24, a 10% increase) for quality-sensitive gift purchasers. A 10% peak-season increase could be profitable if sales declined less than 22.2% (9,990 units), since peak-season customers are more sensitive to quality than price.

### Cannibalization and Price Fencing

The chapter addresses **opportunity cost of forgone revenues from cannibalization**. When offering discounted prices, companies must consider whether they're simply shifting sales from higher-priced to lower-priced offerings. **Price fencing**—limiting discounted prices to specific customer segments—can minimize cannibalization. For example, student rush discounts with fencing (limited to bona fide full-time students) can maintain higher prices for most patrons while increasing overall profit.

### Competitive Pricing Analysis

The chapter explains **reactive pricing analysis**: determining minimum sales loss that justifies matching a competitor's price cut. If a competitor cuts price by 15%, the same breakeven formula applies. Ritter's analysis identified two competitor types: local growers (Mathews Nursery) with similar costs and limited peak-season capacity, and distant high-volume suppliers with higher shipping costs. This competitive understanding informed whether to match price cuts.

### Integration with Market Demand

The chapter explains the relationship between breakeven sales analysis and **price elasticity of demand**. Rather than asking "What is the firm's demand elasticity?", the approach asks "What is the minimum demand elasticity required?" to justify a pricing decision. The breakeven sales curve resembles a traditional downward-sloping demand curve from economic theory.

A critical principle: **"Customers in the marketplace, not costs, determine what a product can sell for."** Managers must estimate how much customers will pay before incurring costs. Every cost is incremental and avoidable at some time—even product development and design costs before the design process begins. Once costs become sunk, they should never drive pricing upward.

## Key Points

- Internal financial and external market considerations are often antagonistic forces requiring trade-offs
- Margin goals, return on capital goals, and operating profit goals are irrelevant to pricing decisions after investment is made
- Only incremental, avoidable costs determine the relative profitability of different pricing strategies
- Variable costs are always incremental; fixed costs are only incremental if directly affected by the pricing decision
- Prices below fully allocated average cost can be profitable when costs are not incremental to additional sales
- Replacement cost (NIFO), not historical cost (LIFO), should guide pricing decisions
- Breakeven sales analysis calculates the minimum sales change required to maintain profitability after a price change
- Capital costs are incremental only when capacity is fully utilized
- Price fencing minimizes cannibalization of higher-priced sales
- Customers in the marketplace, not costs, determine what a product can sell for

## Important Examples and Stories

### Music Festival Case Study
A town business manager increased ticket prices from $90 to $100, causing attendance to drop from 1,000 to 914 tickets, generating $7,260 profit but hurting civic pride and local merchants. Analysis revealed that much of the festival's $15,000 planning overhead and $40,000 performer compensation was not incremental to additional sales. Proposal A (Friday performance at $85) estimated 700 Friday tickets with 200 cannibalized Saturday tickets, yielding 500 net increase and $39,500 incremental revenue against $8,000 staging cost. Proposal B (student rush at $85) estimated 100 student rush tickets with 20 cannibalized full-price tickets, yielding 80 net increase and $3,000 incremental revenue.

### Westside Manufacturing (Pillow Company)
Baseline: 4,000 units at $10.00 per unit, $5.50 variable cost, $15,000 fixed costs, $18,000 contribution. Considering 5% price cut to $9.50:
- Price effect: $2,000 loss
- Breakeven: 500 additional units (12.5% increase)
- With $0.22 variable cost reduction from synthetic filler: 265 units (6.6% increase) needed
- 15% price cut to $8.50 requires 70% volume increase to 6,800 units
- 15% price increase to $11.50 allows 25% volume decrease to 3,000 units

### Ritter & Sons (Potted Plants)
Examined pricing for potted chrysanthemums with $7,000 fixed labor, $44,850 ($0.52/unit) variable labor, and $9,000 annual greenhouse cost. Peak season (45,000 capacity): $1.80/mum capital cost (incremental). Off-peak seasons (41,250 combined units): $0 capital cost (non-incremental). Proposed 10% peak-season increase to $4.24 profitable if sales decline less than 22.2% (6,570 units). Proposed 5% off-peak cut to $3.65 requires 9.3% volume increase to break even. Implemented segmented pricing with peak increases and off-peak discounts for large buyers, plus two-tier quality approach.

### Industrial Equipment Examples
- Crane manufacturer doing milling work when vertical turret lathes unused: equipment cost not incremental
- Airlines flying weekend flights not covering proportionate capital costs: weekend fares add more to profits than higher weekday fares
- Packaged goods manufacturers supplying generic versions below average cost with little incremental capital, shipping, and selling costs

## Definitions and Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Incremental costs** | Costs associated with changes in pricing and sales that rise or fall in total when prices change |
| **Variable costs** | Costs of doing business that change with the amount of business (e.g., raw materials) |
| **Fixed costs** | Costs of being in business that may not change with sales volume (e.g., product design, advertising, overheads) |
| **Incremental fixed costs** | Fixed costs that directly result from implementing a price change or offering a version of a product at a different price level |
| **Semifixed costs** | Costs that are fixed over a range of sales but vary when sales go outside that range |
| **Profit contribution** | Revenue minus incremental, avoidable costs |
| **Avoidable costs** | Costs that either have not yet been incurred or can be reversed (selling costs, delivery, inventory replacement, rental costs) |
| **Sunk costs** | Costs that a company is irreversibly committed to bear (past R&D, rent within current lease term) |
| **Relevant costs** | Costs that actually determine the profit impact of a pricing decision |
| **NIFO accounting** | Next-in, first-out; uses future replacement costs for managerial decision-making |
| **LIFO accounting** | Last-in, first-out; uses historical costs (inappropriate for pricing decisions) |
| **Breakeven sales change** | The minimum increase in sales volume necessary for a price cut to produce an increase in contribution, or maximum decrease for a price increase |
| **Price effect** | The loss in contribution on sales that could have been made at the higher price |
| **Volume effect** | The contribution earned from increased sales volume resulting from a price reduction |
| **Price fencing** | Limiting discounted prices to specific customer segments to minimize cannibalization |
| **Opportunity cost** | Revenues forgone from other sales when cheaper alternatives are offered |
| **Price elasticity of demand** | Percent change in quantity demanded for a given percent change in price |
| **Breakeven sales curve** | A chart summarizing results of a series of breakeven sales analyses for different price changes |
