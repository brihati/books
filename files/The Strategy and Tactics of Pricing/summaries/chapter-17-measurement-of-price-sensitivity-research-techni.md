# Chapter 8: Measurement of Price Sensitivity—Research Techniques to Supplement Judgment

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter provides a comprehensive framework for measuring customer price sensitivity through quantitative and qualitative research methods. The author emphasizes that while numerical estimates can substantially improve pricing decisions, they are approximations that must be interpreted carefully and integrated with managerial judgment.

### Research Design Framework

The chapter classifies measurement procedures along two key dimensions:

1. **Conditions of Measurement**: ranging from uncontrolled (researchers observe actual behavior) to highly controlled (researchers manipulate variables)
2. **Variables Measured**: actual purchases versus purchase preferences/intentions

The fundamental trade-off is between accuracy and cost/realism. Controlled research generally produces more accurate estimates but is costly and less realistic, while uncontrolled research is cheaper and more realistic but less accurate.

### Historical Data Analysis Methods

Three primary data sources enable price sensitivity estimation:

**Historical Sales Data** provides aggregated sales reports but masks individual store price differences and consumer segments with varying price sensitivity. A manufacturer's sales data only reflects price elasticity at the next channel level, not end-user behavior.

**Panel Data** offers superior granularity through individual purchase reports from consumer panels. Panel participation increased dramatically from under 5% to over 70% with scanner technology, reducing bias from heightened price awareness. Panel data allows correlation of price sensitivity with demographic classifications for market segmentation.

**Store Scanner Data** provides high-frequency, immediate information on product movement at individual retail outlets. Combined with loyalty cards and online profiles, transaction data enables connection to specific consumers for segment analysis.

**Limitations of Historical Analysis**: Statistical techniques cannot reveal price effects if there has been little historical price variation. Multi-collinearity occurs when variables like advertising are always changed with price, making it difficult to isolate price impact. Researchers must ensure sufficient historical price variation exists before attempting multivariate regression analysis.

### Experimental Research Methods

**In-Store Purchase Experiments** control marketing variables while maintaining realistic purchase decisions. Quaker Oats conducted a major in-store experiment requiring 120 stores over three months at a cost of several million dollars. These experiments provide fairly reliable estimates to the degree the experimental setting reflects the actual purchase environment.

**Laboratory Purchase Experiments** attempt to duplicate in-store realism without high costs or competitor exposure. Proportionate sampling ensures the experimental population represents actual buyer demographics. Sophisticated simulated stores the size of small convenience stores can better replicate actual shopping conditions than simple laboratory settings.

**Online Laboratory Experiments** have become increasingly cost-effective, completing in as little as one week at one-tenth the cost of other environments. A case study of an online retailer testing e-book pricing with 2,000 respondents revealed that e-book demand is relatively price inelastic below $9.99 but very elastic above $9.99 for titles generally available anywhere.

### Survey Research Techniques

**Buy-Response Surveys** ask consumers if they would purchase at a specific price, yielding more plausible answers than open-ended price questions. These surveys create purchase probability curves showing how willingness-to-buy changes across price points. However, buy-response data should not be treated as directly predictive of actual sales due to consumer bias and imperfect knowledge of competitor prices.

**Direct Questioning** about willingness-to-pay yields unreliable results due to bargaining behavior and desire to please researchers. Uncontrolled direct questioning should never be accepted as valid methodology for estimating price sensitivity.

**In-Depth Interviews** are semi-structured methods lasting one to two hours that infer value from product usage patterns rather than asking directly about price. Approximately 90% of key customer needs are uncovered with 10-12 interviews per major market segment. These interviews are particularly valuable for complex business-to-business pricing research but are expensive and require specialized interviewers.

**Evocative Anchoring** asks respondents to identify budget items they might trade-off for proposed value, helping estimate the value of customer retention or cost savings.

**Simulated Purchase Experiments** overcome drawbacks of other survey types by approximating actual purchase processes. Consumers cannot easily treat these as bargaining positions since they don't know which brand interests the researcher. These experiments provide quick, low-cost information on consumer buying behavior.

**Trade-Off (Conjoint) Analysis** disaggregates a product's price into values consumers attach to each attribute, enabling product design and market segmentation. This technique reveals underlying preferences rather than directly stated intentions and allows prediction of consumer preferences for attribute combinations not currently in the marketplace. However, online conjoint surveys may overestimate price sensitivity due to side-by-side price comparisons and may not accurately simulate purchases typically negotiated in person (homes, restaurant meals, B2B transactions).

### Case Study: Blue Sky Ski Company

A market research study of 1,200 skiers across North America tested pricing for a ski with vibration control technology. Initial analysis showed disappointing results with revenue maximization at $450 and small market share. However, segmentation analysis revealed the "innovators" segment (35-50 year old men who were good skiers in youth) could support an $800 price point with higher take rates and lower advertising costs through specialty shops. Moving from a 90-day to a one-year warranty more than doubled the take rate in the target segment.

### Integration of Managerial Judgment

The chapter emphasizes that estimating price sensitivity is both "art" and "science." Managerial judgment should:

- Guide research design, sample selection, and data analysis
- Identify seasonal patterns, holidays, and purchase cycles affecting historical data
- Separate confounded variables like price deals and promotional displays
- Determine focus on target demographic groups
- Reduce random error and solve statistical problems in historical data analysis
- Evaluate whether results align with expectations

Managers should begin any price sensitivity study by collecting information about buyers—who they are, why they buy, and how they make purchase decisions. Qualitative research should precede quantitative measurement efforts.

### Selection of Appropriate Techniques

The appropriate measurement technique depends on the product's development stage:

- **Concept/Prototype Stage**: Trade-off analysis and preference research
- **Market-Ready Products**: In-store or laboratory purchase experiments
- **Established Products**: Actual purchase data from historical analysis

For frequently purchased, low-cost products, in-store experiments or simulated purchase surveys adjusted for bias are appropriate. For high-cost durables, simple laboratory experiments or simulated purchase surveys may be reasonably accurate since consumers naturally focus on price.

## Key Points

- Quantitative estimates of customer price sensitivity can substantially improve price setting and price segmentation
- Precision in estimates does not necessarily mean accuracy; even precise estimates are only approximations
- Controlled research generally produces more accurate estimates but is costly and less realistic
- Actual-purchase studies are more desirable than preference-intention studies but are more costly and time-consuming
- Historical data analysis has become important due to increased use of scanners, databases, and user profiles
- Panel data is superior to aggregate sales data due to more observations from shorter, more comparable time periods
- Multi-collinearity and insufficient price variation limit historical data analysis effectiveness
- Laboratory experiments cost only a small fraction of in-store testing
- Buy-response surveys are superior to direct questioning for measuring price sensitivity
- Trade-off analysis can identify which product attributes consumers value and how much
- Segmentation analysis can reveal profitable opportunities in niche markets despite disappointing overall results
- Managerial judgment and empirical estimates are complementary, not mutually exclusive
- Numerical estimates should supplement, not replace, subjective managerial judgment

## Important Examples and Stories

**Laundry Detergent Research**: Marketing researchers collecting data on consumer purchases in a grocery store illustrate uncontrolled measurement, while controlled studies where researchers select prices, advertising, and shelf placement demonstrate controlled measurement.

**Tractor Manufacturer**: Created an internal competitive sales database where sales force registered competitive bid information, enabling price sensitivity estimation by customer segment with a $50,000 investment.

**Motel 6**: Uses electronic billboards to change prices by the hour at nearly no cost, allowing study of price responsiveness by location, day of week, and time of day.

**Gigwalk**: Provides cloud-based workforce for gathering in-store prices, sourcing competitive information, and collecting market insights.

**Quaker Oats In-Store Experiment**: Required 120 stores, ran for three months, and cost several million dollars to study price effects.

**E-Book Pricing Study**: An online retailer engaged a research firm to design an online laboratory experiment with 2,000 respondents. The market research company spent one-and-a-half weeks designing the experiment and recruiting respondents from email lists of electronic book purchasers. Results showed e-book demand was price inelastic below $9.99 but elastic above $9.99 for widely available titles, leading to a segmented pricing model.

**Financial Management Software**: A software company conducted a national study on a financial management program. An online survey of 1,800 consumers evaluating software at prices ranging from $100 to $500 showed that at $100, 58% of consumers indicated they would purchase; at $200, fewer respondents were willing to buy; from $300 to $500, the proportion willing to buy changed little.

**Blue Sky Ski Company**: Commissioned a market research study of 1,200 skiers across North America for a ski with vibration control technology. Initial analysis showed disappointing results with revenue maximization at $450. However, segmentation analysis revealed the "innovators" segment (35-50 year old men who were good skiers in youth) could support an $800 price point with higher take rates and lower advertising costs through specialty shops.

## Definitions and Terms

**Uncontrolled Measurements**: Researchers are only observers measuring what people actually do or say they would do in a situation not of the researcher's making.

**Controlled Measurements**: Researchers manipulate important variables that influence consumer behavior to more precisely observe their effect.

**Actual-Purchase Studies**: Measure behavior in real or simulated purchase situations.

**Preference-Intention Studies**: Measure intended choices that people claim they would make in a hypothetical purchase situation.

**Historical Sales Data**: Sales reports from a company's own records or from a sales-monitoring service.

**Panel Data**: Individual purchase reports from members of a consumer panel, typically a few thousand households.

**Store Scanner Data**: Sales data for an individual retail outlet.

**Price Elasticity**: The responsiveness of sales to price changes at a particular channel level.

**Multi-Collinearity**: When another variable (such as advertising) is always changed along with price, making it difficult to disentangle impacts.

**In-Store Purchase Experiment**: Research relying on actual purchase data collected when buyers are unaware they are participating in an experiment.

**Laboratory Purchase Experiment**: Experiments that attempt to duplicate the realism of in-store experimentation without high cost or possible exposure to competitors.

**Proportionate Sampling**: A technique to control the proportion of participants in each demographic classification to ensure the experimental population is representative of the actual population of buyers.

**Purchase Probability Curve**: A demand curve for market share created by aggregating answers from different consumers at different price levels in buy-response surveys.

**Halo Effects**: Tendency of respondents to not carefully discriminate among listed attributes and give similar ratings to many attributes, especially those adjacent to each other.

**In-Depth Interview**: A semi-structured method used to elicit responses from customers on how they use products and services, from which researchers infer value rather than asking about value directly.

**Evocative Anchoring**: A method used in business-to-business markets that asks respondents to identify items in their budget that they might consider a trade-off to obtain the value and benefits promised by a supplier's proposed product or service solution.

**Simulated Purchase Experiment**: A research technique where respondents are asked to imagine they are on a shopping trip or sent to a simulated store (physical or virtual) to conduct a shopping exercise, choosing among brands at various prices.

**Trade-Off (Conjoint) Analysis**: An experimental technique that measures price sensitivity and sensitivity to other product attributes by disaggregating a product's price into the values consumers attach to each attribute.

**Multinomial Logit Model**: Statistical model used to evaluate impact of promotional activities on consumer product choice.

**Confidence Interval**: A range within which we may have some degree of statistical certainty that the true estimate of price sensitivity lies.
