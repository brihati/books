# Chapter 5: Pricing Policy Influencing Customer Expectations and Purchase Behaviors

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter examines how pricing policies—rules and conditions for price discounts or surcharges applied consistently to customers or transactions—shape customer expectations and purchasing behavior. The core argument is that customer expectations about future price changes, driven by past pricing behavior, determine willingness-to-pay more than the actual price itself.

### Foundations: Price Structure vs. Pricing Policy

The chapter distinguishes between **price structure** (macro-level alignment of price differences with value differences and cost to serve across customer segments) and **pricing policy** (rules defining when and how prices change within segments). A pricing policy is "a rule or habit, consistently applied, that defines the criteria under which a company will change a price for an individual customer, for a limited period of time or for particular transactions."

### How Pricing Policies Create Customer Expectations

Periodic, predictable discounting trains customers to wait for sales, reducing regular-price sales. Professional B-to-B buyers delay purchases until late quarter when sales managers discount to meet goals. Reactive pricing policies that match competitor prices create expectations that competitive bidding will be rewarded. Sellers often misinterpret declining regular-price sales as increased price sensitivity when customers are actually responding to incentives created by pricing policies.

### Defending Against Professional Purchasing Tactics

Professional purchasers use four tactics to disconnect price from value:

1. **Commoditizing offers through specs and reverse auctions**: Customers distribute exact specifications for competitors to bid against. Reverse auctions allow bidders to see others' prices and revise bids electronically. Sellers should either refuse to participate or bid list prices to force honest Give-Get negotiation.

2. **Double discounting of price increases**: Large buyers demand discounts on price increases because they already enjoy volume discounts. This is a bogus argument that compounds costs over time. A 6% price increase becomes 4.5% for a buyer with a 25% volume discount, yet the buyer argues they should receive only the discounted increase.

3. **Across-the-board price increases with volume discount complications**: When increasing prices, apply increases uniformly without exceptions. If concessions are needed for powerful buyers, use alternatives like delayed implementation rather than discounting the increase itself.

4. **Discounting to compensate for past failures**: Lump-sum compensation or time-limited invoice credits preserve pricing integrity better than price concessions. One client saw prices for volume buyers drop to less than 50% of list price in seven years due to repeated discount demands.

### Managing Different Buyer Types

The chapter identifies four buyer types requiring different policy responses:

**Value-driven buyers** are sophisticated purchasing departments consolidating large volumes. They can afford to search and evaluate alternatives and try to manage both benefits and push down price. For these buyers, implement pre-approved value trade-offs and discount policies. Design policies enabling customers to change purchasing behavior to focus on understanding what creates value. Create unbundled service options as low-cost alternatives to price concessions.

**Brand-driven buyers** value differentiation but find evaluation costs too high. Price objections signal either service disappointment or market price awareness. Address service failures through recompense and service improvements, not price reductions. Never let price premiums for relationship buyers exceed the value being delivered.

**Price-driven buyers** genuinely seek only specified levels of features or services, using sealed bid or reverse auction processes. Strip out every cost not required to meet minimum specifications. Design low-cost offers to be unattractive to existing customers. Never use promotional pricing to convert price buyers to value buyers; it reinforces price-buying behavior.

**Convenience buyers** don't compare prices and buy from the easiest source, willing to pay premiums for convenience on small or infrequent purchases.

**Power buyers** control significant volume and expect better prices than other customers. Examples include Walmart and Home Depot. Reactive dealing with power buyers almost certainly results in profitability decline. Make power buyers compete by offering exclusive products or services that drive store traffic. Quantify the differential value your brand brings to power buyers. Resist divide-and-conquer tactics by setting your own pricing policies with conditions tied to full-line approval.

### The Give-Get Negotiation Principle

No price concession should be made without getting something from the other side. Testing trade-offs reveals whether objections stem from lack of value or expectation of concessions. If you never test give-get tactics, you never know whether objections are driven by lack of value or expectation of concessions. By proposing trade-offs, you can learn what value buyers actually value.

A printing industry case study illustrates this principle: When a printer failed to deliver a catalog on time for seasonal merchandise, the sales rep wanted to slash price. Instead, the printer's president proposed a premium service where the customer could purchase priority status in the plant with a financial guarantee. The printer offered a large credit in the first year of a three-year contract as an apology. The customer accepted a 24% premium over their already high rate and expressed appreciation for the respect shown to their relationship.

### Price Integrity and the Downward Spiral

**Price integrity** is the belief that the initially quoted price is actually a fair market price that other customers pay for the same thing. Ad hoc discounting undermines price integrity. When price integrity is lost, purchasing agents are motivated to exploit it through aggressive negotiation tactics. Buyers shift negotiation from seller managing expectations to buyer managing expectations. Flexible price negotiation leads customers to solicit bids from competitors and diversify suppliers. Erosion of price integrity causes sellers to believe products have become commoditized and discount more.

A sales rep saying he cannot change pricing without manager approval communicates that the company makes price concessions, requires escalation to get them, and the customer is speaking to the wrong person. This creates a downward spiral where lack of consistent policies for responding to price resistance creates expectations that customers can negotiate lower prices.

### Implementing Price Increases

Before announcing a price increase, publicly communicate why the increase is necessary for the industry based on costs or capacity demands. Announce the size, effective date, and specific product lines affected, explaining the cause-and-effect relationship. Empower important customers with transition guarantees ensuring they won't be disadvantaged relative to competitors. Never back off full increases for resistant customers while leaving loyal customers to pay full amount. If a major competitor fails to implement comparable increases, proactively contact customers to explain temporary suspension of the increase.

Customers fear being first to accept price increases and losing competitive advantage. Transition guarantees that match competitor concessions help customers accept increases without fear of competitive disadvantage. Consistent application of price increases across all customers builds trust, while selective discounting for resistant customers undermines loyalty.

### Transitioning to Policy-Based Pricing

Transitioning from flexible to policy-based pricing requires time and careful planning rather than rigid implementation. Use **price banding** to identify illegitimate price variation on aggregate and per-account basis. Address **outlaw accounts** (customers with unjustifiably low prices) first as they are least profitable and pull down other customers. Have senior management communicate pricing corrections to customers, not sales representatives. Offer phased price increases as part of transition agreements (e.g., one-third immediately, one-third in six months, one-third in 12 months). Be willing to walk away from genuine price buyers to signal commitment to profitable partnerships.

### Promotional Pricing Policies

Promotional discounts can depress future willingness-to-pay and encourage customer stockpiling. Coupons and rebates can limit stockpiling by loyal customers and target prospective buyers. Money-back guarantees and performance-based rebates reduce trial risk without cutting price.

For service companies, never use discounts on service price to induce trial. Customers perceive returning to higher prices after a discount period as a loss, causing them to switch providers. Once someone spends 6-12 months enjoying a service at one price, renewing it at a higher price is viewed as a "loss" to be resisted. Instead, use non-price incentives like free gifts that maintain price integrity and prevent loss perception at contract renewal.

During economic downturns, avoid using price to take market share from close competitors to prevent price wars. Instead, promote lower-priced product alternatives. Use indexed pricing tied to customer market conditions to support customers while maintaining automatic price increase mechanisms. Unbundle product elements to allow cost reduction without cutting base prices.

### Building Competitive Advantage Through Policies

Analyzing pricing challenges and developing policies is an ongoing process and responsibility of pricing staff and managers with collective responsibility for profitability. Creating policies cannot be the responsibility of sales management alone due to lack of market perspective and authority for trade-offs. Pricing by policy forces companies to consider the impact on the entire market when making a pricing decision and reflects the "shadow of the future."

Over time, a company's policies can become a source of competitive advantage—creating expectations that drive better behavior on the part of customers, competitors, and sales reps. Policies empower sales representatives to offer creative solutions more quickly with less internal selling effort. Building an effective set of policies takes time.

## Key Points

- Pricing policies shape customer expectations more than actual prices
- Periodic, predictable discounting trains customers to wait for sales
- Professional purchasers use tactics to disconnect price from value that require defensive pricing policies
- Price integrity—belief that quoted prices are fair market prices—is essential to pricing power
- Four buyer types (value-driven, brand-driven, price-driven, convenience-driven) and power buyers require different policy approaches
- Give-get negotiation ensures price concessions require something in return
- Ad hoc discounting creates downward spiral of eroding margins and commoditization
- Transparent, consistent policies prevent customers from gaming the system
- Price increases must be applied uniformly without exceptions
- Transition to policy-based pricing requires time, management support, and addressing outlaw accounts first
- Non-price incentives maintain price integrity better than discounts for trial induction
- Strong pricing policies become competitive advantage by creating customer expectations

## Important Examples and Stories

**Predictable Discounting**: Retail consumer delays purchase of fall fashion expecting a 20%-off sale within a month based on store's past behavior. Professional B-to-B buyers hold purchases until last two weeks of quarter when sales managers discount to achieve quarterly goals.

**Strategic Sourcing by Buyers**: Purchasing agents split purchases among multiple suppliers (60% to best price, 30% to second best, 10% to third best) and regularly switch rankings to induce bidding wars.

**Amazon's Subscribe-and-Save**: Applies increasing discounts for ordering more unique items together regularly, but not for ordering more units of the same item.

**Price Increase Compounding**: One client saw its prices for volume buyers drop to less than 50% of list price in only seven years due to repeated discount demands. A buyer offering 10% more volume in return for a 2% price discount is actually getting a 22% price cut on the incremental volume.

**Printing Industry Case Study**: A printer failed to deliver a catalog on time for seasonal merchandise. Instead of slashing price, the printer's president proposed a premium service where the customer could purchase priority status in the plant with a financial guarantee. The printer offered a large credit in the first year of a three-year contract as an apology. The customer accepted a 24% premium over their already high rate and expressed appreciation for the respect shown to their relationship.

**Power Buyer Tactics**: A large medical products company confronted with divide-and-conquer tactics returned multiple bid forms for each product with different prices, specifying conditions under which each price applied—lowest if all products approved, highest if only subset approved. When a hospital buying group rejected this strategy, the seller maintained its policy and explained how the channel value was vastly reduced without complete product line acceptance. Recognizing the cost of losing all the seller's products with large market share, the buying group approved all products.

**Exclusive Products Strategy**: Benjamin Moore paints maintain high value to local hardware stores because they are not available at Home Depot or Lowe's. Target offers exclusive designs from famous designers in jewelry, women's accessories, and home goods.

**Recession Pricing**: Winn-Dixie promoted lower-priced brands during recession and weathered it with less damage to profitability than competitors engaged in price wars. A large supermarket chain initiated a price war during recession that increased revenue share but tanked profit share.

**Trial Incentives**: Bowflex does not discount its high-end exercise equipment but combines direct-to-customer value communication with a money-back guarantee within the first six weeks of delivery. Velcade cancer treatment was rejected by NHS as not cost-effective, but the company offered a performance-based guarantee to refund the entire cost for patients not showing adequate improvement.

## Definitions and Terms

**Price Structure**: Establishing alignment of price differences with value differences and cost to serve across macro segments of customers and applications.

**Pricing Policy**: A rule or habit, consistently applied, that defines the criteria under which a company will change a price for an individual customer, for a limited period of time or for particular transactions.

**Price Integrity**: The belief that the initially quoted price is actually a fair market price that other customers pay for the same thing.

**Give-Get Negotiation**: A principle where no price concession is made without getting something from the other side.

**Strategic Sourcing**: A sophisticated process and policy used by large purchasing departments to ensure they get the lowest prices possible while establishing conditions that increase future leverage.

**Reverse Auction**: Electronic bidding where bids are open for everyone to see others' prices but not bidder identities, allowing bid revision.

**Price Banding**: A technique that enables managers to estimate how much of the price variation is illegitimate.

**Outlaws**: Customers who enjoy prices much lower than other customers for the same products, service levels, and commitments.

**At-Risks**: Customers paying more than can be justified relative to the average.

**Value-driven buyers**: Sophisticated purchasing departments that consolidate and buy large volumes, can afford cost to search and evaluate alternatives, trying to manage both benefits and push down price.

**Brand-driven buyers**: Those for whom differentiation is valuable but the cost to evaluate all suppliers is too high.

**Price-driven buyers**: Those genuinely not looking for features or services exceeding specified levels, using sealed bid or reverse auction processes.

**Convenience-driven buyers**: Those who don't compare prices and buy from the easiest source, willing to pay premiums for convenience on small or infrequent purchases.

**Power buyers**: Buyers who control so much volume that they have the power to deliver or deny huge amounts of market share.

**Indexed Pricing**: Pricing tied to customer market conditions that supports customers during difficult times while enabling automatic increases when conditions improve.
