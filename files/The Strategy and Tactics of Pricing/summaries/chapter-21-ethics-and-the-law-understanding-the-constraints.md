# Chapter 12: Ethics and the Law—Understanding the Constraints on Pricing

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter examines the ethical and legal constraints that shape pricing decisions, presenting a comprehensive framework for understanding when and how managers can ethically and legally pursue profit through pricing strategies.

### Ethical Constraints on Pricing

The chapter establishes a topology of five ethical levels that range from minimal to maximum constraints on pricing:

**Ethical Level One** requires that all transactions be voluntary. This foundational principle prohibits coercive pricing arrangements such as organized crime protection schemes or forced purchases from company stores in one-company towns.

**Ethical Level Two** condemns transactions involving unequal information. This level holds that sellers have natural information advantages and may have ethical duties to disclose completely and accurately. Examples include selling used cars without disclosing known defects, selling Florida land at inflated prices to unaware out-of-state buyers, and selling lottery tickets to people unlikely to recognize their poor returns.

**Ethical Level Three** restricts profit on necessities with limited alternatives. This level recognizes that when customers have few choices for essential goods or services, pricing power becomes ethically problematic. Examples include raising ice prices during power failures and raising lumber prices following hurricanes.

**Ethical Level Four** extends ethical restrictions to all products and justifies profit only as the minimum necessary to induce suppliers to improve the well-being of less fortunate members of society. Under this standard, economic rents—profits from exploiting unique skills, great ideas, or exceptional efficiency—are morally suspect unless everyone benefits. Segmented pricing is condemned unless price differences reflect cost differences.

**Ethical Level Five**, based on the Marxist principle "From each according to his ability, to each according to his need," is inconsistent with markets and can thrive only within families, small self-selected societies, and organizations with defined bounds.

The chapter emphasizes that managers must clarify their own ethical standards and their customers' expectations before ambiguous situations arise, and must consider how family, neighbors, and social groups might view pricing decisions.

### Legal Framework: U.S. Antitrust Law

U.S. antitrust law has evolved over 125+ years with a trend toward greater pricing freedom, though with necessary ambiguity. The legal system has shifted from judging behavior based on economic assumptions to emphasizing demonstrable economic effect. The chapter notes that attorneys often give overly conservative advice on antitrust matters.

**Enforcement Mechanisms**: The Department of Justice, Federal Trade Commission, and private parties enforce antitrust laws. Private antitrust litigation volume dwarfs government-brought cases. Private parties may seek injunctions and treble damages (three times actual economic damages).

**Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002)**: This legislation, enacted in response to corporate finance scandals (Enron, WorldCom), made antitrust compliance more rigorous by requiring stricter financial and auditing procedures. CFOs and CEOs must certify financial reporting documents; knowing certification of non-compliant financials is a criminal offense. The act's broad scope affects pricing practices even though it was not created expressly to police antitrust compliance.

### Price Fixing: Horizontal and Vertical

**Horizontal Price Fixing** occurs when competitors agree on prices or key terms of sale affecting price. Direct horizontal price fixing remains per se illegal (easier to prove than rule of reason offenses). However, conscious parallelism alone—imitating a competitor's pricing behavior—is not illegal; illegal concerted activity requires conscious parallelism plus "plus factors."

**Vertical Price Fixing** involves agreements between suppliers and resellers on resale prices. The legal landscape shifted significantly with two Supreme Court decisions:

- **Khan (1997)**: Applied rule of reason analysis to maximum price agreements
- **Leegin (2007)**: Extended rule of reason to minimum and exact resale prices

**The Colgate Doctrine (1919)** permits unilateral resale price setting without agreement. Suppliers can announce prices at which products must be resold and refuse to sell to non-compliant resellers, as long as there is no agreement. Suppliers can refuse to sell to resellers not complying with resale price policies.

**Incidental Price Fixing**: When a restriction on price is merely the incidental effect of a desirable pro-competitive activity, it is judged under rule of reason rather than per se illegality.

### Resale Price Encouragement and MAP Programs

Suppliers can use legal strategies to encourage desired pricing without per se illegality:

**Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) Programs** provide advertising allowances in return for adhering to appropriate prices in advertising, catalogs, or internet. However, MAP programs that suppress virtually all forms of price communication, are virtually identical across an industry, and have demonstrated adverse effects on industry pricing lack procompetitive justification and may be challenged.

**Reseller's Choice Approach**: Resellers may choose to sell at supplier-set prices or refuse without triggering per se illegal price fixing.

**Direct Dealing Programs**: Suppliers can sell directly to end-users or constructively through agents, with resellers acting as warehousing and delivery agents compensated only for these functions.

### Robinson-Patman Act: Price and Promotional Discrimination

The Robinson-Patman Act (1936) amended existing legislation to protect small businesses from discriminatory pricing. The act applies to goods only, not services.

**Five Elements of Illegal Price Discrimination**:
1. Sale of goods
2. Products of like grade and quality
3. Different prices charged to different customers
4. Sales in interstate commerce
5. Competitive injury (primary-line or secondary-line)

**Three Defenses to Price Discrimination**:
1. **Cost Justification**: Price disparity based on legitimate cost differences. Requires regular updates to cost analysis. The law permits passing on some or all actual savings to customers, but not a penny more.
2. **Meeting Competition**: Good-faith belief that discriminatory price is necessary to meet a competitive supplier's price. Can be applied at micro or macro level. Relies on purchaser-provided competitive information, though buyers have incentive to lie.
3. **Changing Conditions**: Permits special prices for perishable, seasonal, obsolete, or distressed merchandise.

**Promotional Discrimination** (Sections 2(d) and 2(e)) relates to how resellers sell supplier products, while price discrimination relates to how they buy them. Three elements must be present: provision of allowances/services/facilities, connection with resale of supplier's goods, and unavailability to all competing customers on proportionally equal terms.

**Proportional Equality**: Rather than requiring equal treatment, promotional discrimination law requires "proportional equality," which can be achieved through three methods: unit or dollar purchases, cost to reseller of promotional activity, or value of promotional activity to supplier.

**Functional Discounts**: Trade discounts based on a purchaser's role in the supplier's distribution system may be lawful if they reflect reasonable compensation for services provided.

### Vertical Non-Price Restrictions

The 1977 Sylvania decision gave suppliers flexibility in imposing vertical non-price restrictions subject to rule of reason analysis. These restrictions can reduce intrabrand competition (competition between resellers of the same brand) to promote interbrand competition (rivalry between competing brands).

**Territorial Restrictions** include:
- **Exclusive Distributorship**: Supplier grants a particular reseller as exclusive outlet in a territory or market
- **Absolute Confinement**: Reseller required to sell only in a particular territory or market
- **Airtight Territory**: Combination of exclusive distributorship with absolute confinement
- **Area of Primary Responsibility**: Permits sales outside territory but expects focus on designated area
- **Profit Passover**: Allows sales anywhere but requires splitting revenue/profit for sales outside territory
- **Location Clause**: Restricts reseller to approved sites only

**Product Restrictions**: Suppliers can generally determine which products to sell to resellers or end-users, except when under contract or when a monopolist with excess capacity.

**Exclusive Dealing**: Reseller or end-user not permitted to purchase particular products from another supplier. Judged under rule of reason; permissible if competing suppliers have reasonable market access.

**Tying and Bundling**: Tying requires customers to purchase an undesirable product to obtain a desirable one and requires five elements to be unlawful. Bundling is legal if products are available separately at reasonable costs.

**Full-Line Forcing**: Requires resellers to carry entire product line to prevent cherry-picking.

### Predatory Pricing and Price Signaling

**Predatory Pricing**: Setting prices so low that the seller harms its own profitability to do greater harm to competitors. Requires proof that the seller could recoup losses with higher prices later—a heavy burden of proof. The 1993 Supreme Court ruling severely limits predation claims and favors the presumption that price-cutting is procompetitive.

**Price Signaling**: Lawful unless it is collusive, predatory, coercive, or exclusionary. Can serve legitimate business purposes in aiding buyer planning. However, price signaling involving non-public data exchange can be treated as equivalent to direct price-fixing discussions.

### Monopolization and Attempted Monopolization

**Monopolization** requires monopoly power in relevant market and willful acquisition or maintenance of that power. Monopolists using loyalty programs to entrench or extend monopoly power can violate Section 2 of the Sherman Act.

**Attempted Monopolization** consists of predatory or exclusionary conduct, specific or predatory intent, and dangerous probability of success.

**Price Squeeze**: Where an integrated manufacturer with large market share in a key input sells it at higher price to competing manufacturers than it sells its own finished products.

### Enforcement Actions and Consequences

Criminal antitrust violations can result in fines up to $100 million for corporations and up to $1 million or ten years imprisonment for individuals. Civil cases can result in injunctions, civil penalties, treble damages, and attorney fees. Defense against antitrust claims is disruptive and expensive even when successful.

## Key Points

- Pricing decisions must balance profitability with ethical perception and legality
- Five distinct ethical levels exist regarding pricing constraints, from voluntary transactions only to redistributive principles
- U.S. antitrust law has evolved toward greater pricing freedom based on demonstrable economic effect rather than assumptions
- Direct horizontal price fixing remains per se illegal; vertical price fixing is now subject to rule of reason analysis post-Leegin (2007)
- The Colgate doctrine permits unilateral resale price setting without agreement
- Robinson-Patman Act prohibits price and promotional discrimination with three available defenses
- Vertical non-price restrictions are subject to rule of reason analysis post-Sylvania (1977)
- Predatory pricing requires proof of pricing below cost and dangerous probability of achieving monopoly power
- Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) made antitrust compliance more rigorous through financial and auditing requirements
- Contemporary antitrust law provides considerable flexibility to develop alternative pricing strategies

## Important Examples and Stories

**Ethical Violations**:
- Sale of protection by organized crime
- Forcing employees in one-company towns to buy from company stores
- Trusts using reprehensible tactics to drive lower-priced competitors out of business
- Selling used cars without disclosing known defects
- Selling Florida land at inflated prices to unaware out-of-state buyers
- Selling lottery tickets to people unlikely to recognize poor returns
- Raising ice prices during power failures
- Raising lumber prices following hurricanes

**Legal Cases and Enforcement Actions**:
- **United States v. Andreas** (Archer Daniels Midland executives): Direct price fixing
- **United States v. Container Corp.**: Indirect price fixing
- **Chicago Board of Trade v. United States**: Exchange rule requiring after-hours trading at most recent market close prices
- **Herman Miller case** (2008): Settled by consent decree four days after filing by Attorneys General of New York, Michigan, and Illinois alleging illegal minimum price-fixing agreements
- **FTC enforcement against five major music suppliers** (Sony Music, Universal Music, BMG Music, Time Warner, Capitol Records): MAP program violations
- **Pharmaceutical resellers lawsuit**: Settled for more than $700 million alleging preferential pricing to health maintenance and managed care organizations violated Robinson-Patman Act
- **Eight airlines price-fixing settlement**: Involved computerized system for communicating fare changes in advance through jointly owned data collection company
- **National Collegiate Athletic Association v. Board of Regents**: Association rules on athletic equipment standards were appropriate, but limiting television exposure was unreasonable restriction on output

## Definitions and Terms

**Caveat Emptor**: "Let the buyer beware"—a legal principle characterizing nearly all economic transactions in the United States prior to the 20th century

**Economic Rents**: Profits from exploiting unique skills, great ideas, or exceptional efficiency

**Horizontal Price Fixing**: Competitors agree on prices or key terms of sale affecting price

**Vertical Price Fixing**: Supplier and reseller agree on resale prices or price-related terms

**Conscious Parallelism**: Imitating a competitor's pricing behavior; alone, not illegal without "plus factors"

**Incidental Price Fixing**: Restriction on price that is merely the incidental effect of a desirable pro-competitive activity

**Per Se Illegal**: Offenses requiring proof of the objectionable practice, antitrust injury, and damages

**Rule of Reason**: Analysis requiring proof that the practice is unreasonably anticompetitive, with presumption of legality

**Treble Damages**: Three times actual economic damages that private parties may seek in antitrust cases

**Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) Program**: Reseller receives advertising allowance in return for adhering to appropriate price in advertising, catalog, or internet

**Exclusive Distributorship**: Supplier grants a particular reseller as exclusive outlet in territory or market

**Absolute Confinement**: Reseller required to sell only in particular territory or market

**Airtight Territory**: Combination of exclusive distributorship with absolute confinement

**Area of Primary Responsibility**: Permits sales outside territory but expects focus on designated area

**Profit Passover**: Allows sales anywhere but requires splitting revenue/profit for sales outside territory

**Location Clause**: Restricts reseller to approved sites only

**Exclusive Dealing**: Reseller or end-user not permitted to purchase particular products from another supplier

**Tying**: Requires customers to purchase undesirable product to obtain desirable one

**Bundling**: Offering products or services together; legal if available separately

**Full-Line Forcing**: Requires reseller to carry entire product line to prevent cherry-picking

**Predatory Pricing**: Setting prices so low that seller harms own profitability to do greater harm to competitors

**Price Signaling**: Supplier communicating future pricing intentions to competitors

**Monopolization**: Monopoly power in relevant market plus willful acquisition or maintenance of that power

**Attempted Monopolization**: Predatory or exclusionary conduct, specific or predatory intent, and dangerous probability of success

**Price Squeeze**: Integrated manufacturer with large market share sells key input at higher price to competitors than to itself

**Colgate Doctrine**: Permits unilateral resale price setting without agreement

**Proportional Equality**: Promotional benefits need not be equal but must be proportionally equal across competing resellers

**Functional Discount**: Trade discount based on purchaser's role in distribution system reflecting services performed

**Cost Justification**: Defense allowing pricing differences if brand preferences translate into different costs to produce or sell

**Availability Defense**: When discount made available to all or almost all customers but some choose not to take advantage

**Tacit Collusion**: Outcome of oligopolistic market structure; lawful
