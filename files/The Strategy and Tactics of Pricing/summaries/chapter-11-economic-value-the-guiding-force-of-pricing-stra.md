# Chapter 2: Economic Value—The Guiding Force of Pricing Strategy

## Detailed Chapter Summary

Economic value is the foundation of effective pricing strategy. Unlike use value (the total satisfaction a customer receives from a product), economic value—also called exchange value—is determined by what customers' alternatives cost and what differentiates your offering from those alternatives. This distinction is critical: customers will not pay more than the true economic value of a product, making value estimation as essential to pricing as location estimation is to navigation.

### The Economic Value Estimation (EVE) Model

Total economic value equals the reference value (the price of the customer's best alternative) plus the differentiation value (the net benefits delivered over and above that alternative). For example, a cold beverage on a hot beach might have use value of $10, but customers know competing vendors charge $2 half a mile away and convenience stores sell six-packs for $6, so they reject $10 prices as outrageous. The economic value is anchored to these alternatives, not to the product's inherent utility.

Economic value has two forms: **monetary value** (tangible cost savings or revenue increases) and **psychological value** (intangible satisfaction, security, or brand prestige). Business-to-business purchases typically emphasize monetary value, while consumer products often rely more on psychological value. Both require different estimation approaches.

### Identifying Competitive Alternatives and Reference Prices

The critical first step is answering: "If I did not exist, what would my customer do?" This identifies the next-best competitive alternative (NBCA). Competitors may include direct rivals, substitute products, or even customer apathy (doing nothing). Marketers often have myopic views of their markets and fail to identify all alternatives. Customers may construct a basket of products and services as a viable alternative rather than comparing single products.

Competitive reference prices must be measured in units and terms familiar to customers. Raw secondary price data often appears incoherent until normalized for volume and service level differences. For example, a technology manufacturer's competitive pricing data that seemed irrational revealed consistent patterns once adjusted for volume discounts and service variations.

### Estimating Monetary Value

Understanding the customer's business model is essential. Different customer segments may value the same features differently based on how those features affect their profitability. For instance, Distributor Co. served two segments: systems integrators (who valued technical support) and box-pushers (who used free support but dropped usage when charged). The same service had vastly different monetary value to each segment.

Value driver algorithms—simple formulas approximating how value is created—should be developed through in-depth customer interviews. These algorithms quantify the differentiated monetary worth of each unit of product performance. The principle is to "accept being approximately right lest you be precisely wrong in disregarding an important driver of value that seems too difficult to quantify."

**GenetiCorp's Dyna-Test case** illustrates this approach. The DNA testing kit saved 16 hours of processing labor ($384 at $24/hour), eliminated 2 hours of quality control checks ($48), and avoided opportunity costs from sample degradation ($468 average). For industrial buyers, total economic value was $2,528 versus the EnSyn reference price of $30. Academic laboratories, with different business models and lower labor costs, derived only $1,685 in economic value. When GenetiCorp raised prices 2–5 fold while launching aggressive marketing education, sales continued growing because new prices represented only a small fraction of delivered value.

### Estimating Psychological Value

Conjoint analysis, developed in the late 1970s and early 1980s, decomposes products into feature groups and tests customer preferences across various feature sets. This quantitative technique reveals hidden values customers place on product attributes.

**Sport Co.'s golf club case** demonstrates this approach. The company developed the "Big Drive" club with significant improvements in distance and accuracy. A conjoint study of 670 golfers revealed four distinct segments with different value drivers: Innovators (performance-focused frequent golfers), Value Seekers (casual players willing to pay for performance), Lost Players (occasional players who drifted away), and Budget Shoppers (cost-constrained buyers). Warranty emerged as a key value driver across all segments—contrary to initial hypothesis. The optimal price for Innovators was $425 (generating 40,000 unit sales) versus $275 for Budget Shoppers (30% of market). Sport Co. resolved this through a two-tier pricing strategy with minor design and brand modifications.

### The Flaw in Customer Value Modeling (CVM)

Customer Value Modeling, which emerged from the total quality management movement, assumes a single linear relationship between price and perceived quality. This is fundamentally flawed. CVM underestimates the value of more differentiated products and overestimates the value of less differentiated products because it treats reference value and differentiation value as having the same price-to-value ratio. In reality, they have different ratios. Pricing a highly differentiated product at the CVM-hypothesized "fair-value line" will undercharge for the differentiation value.

The value-based price premium one can charge is often much greater than the percentage increase in technical efficiency. A cancer drug 50% more effective should command significantly more than a 50% price premium. A paint sprayer that halves painting time is worth much more than twice the price of a brush.

### Value-Based Market Segmentation

Needs-based segmentation misses customer needs with the greatest operational impact. Value-based segmentation—dividing markets into subgroups whose members have common value drivers—is superior for pricing decisions.

**International Harvester Company's case** illustrates this. IH invested heavily in equipment reliability to match John Deere, but in-depth interviews revealed farmers valued rapid service and downtime avoidance more than reliability itself. Deere's extensive dealer network with spare parts and loaner tractors provided greater value than IH's more reliable equipment.

A six-step framework guides value-based segmentation:

1. **Determine basic segmentation criteria** accounting for both customer needs and company capabilities
2. **Identify discriminating value drivers** that vary most among segments but are homogenous within segments
3. **Conduct in-depth customer interviews** to uncover true value drivers and new product opportunities
4. **Apply activity-based costing** to understand true costs of serving different segments
5. **Develop primary and secondary segmentations** based on critical discriminating power (avoid over-segmentation)
6. **Create segment metrics and fences** to make segmentation actionable

**Commercial printing company example**: The company segmented customers primarily by print timing needs (firm dates vs. flexible scheduling). Firm-date customers subdivided into "brand focus" (custom services), "consistency" (high quality, standard services), and "unique equipment" (special needs). Flexible-scheduling customers split into "cost-conscious" (fixed time window delivery) and "low-touch, low-price" (bare-bones service with internet-to-press transactions).

### Metrics and Fences

Metrics track how customers receive value and how they pay for it. Fences are policies, rules, programs, and structures that qualify customers for discounts or rewards. Examples include minimum volume requirements, time-based membership requirements, and bundled purchase requirements. Car rental companies once used distance-based metrics (charging for mileage plus time) until competition forced them to use time alone. Airlines used Saturday night stay requirements as a fence to separate business travelers from price-sensitive pleasure travelers until competition eliminated the requirement.

### Strategic Implications

Apple's success demonstrates sophisticated management of value perception and realization through advertising, customer support in Apple Stores, avoiding short-term promotional discounts, and offering different models for different segments. Ron Johnson's work at Apple Stores exemplified this value-based approach.

Strategic pricing could accurately be labeled "strategic value management." The goal is not just sales and market share, but profitability. A menu of prices, products, services, and bundles reflecting different value received for different prices paid creates a win-win balance for sellers and buyers.

## Key Points

- Economic value (exchange value) is determined by customer alternatives, not unchanging physical product aspects
- Total economic value = reference value (best alternative price) + differentiation value
- Monetary value drivers are quantifiable cost reductions or revenue increases; psychological value drivers are intangible
- Conjoint analysis quantifies psychological value by testing customer preferences across feature sets
- Value-based segmentation is superior to demographic or needs-based segmentation for pricing
- Reference value and differentiation value have different price-to-value ratios; treating them as a single linear relationship (CVM) is flawed
- Metrics track value delivery; fences qualify customers for discounts or rewards
- Value should be a key input to price-setting decisions, not market share or costs

## Definitions and Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Use value (utility)** | Total savings or satisfaction a customer receives from a product |
| **Economic value (exchange value)** | Value determined by customer alternatives; the key to effective pricing strategy |
| **Consumer surplus** | Extra money left in customers' pockets after paying less than maximum willingness-to-pay |
| **Economic Value Estimation (EVE) Model** | Total economic value = reference value + differentiation value |
| **Reference value** | Price of the customer's best alternative (next-best competitive alternative) |
| **Differentiation value** | Net benefits delivered over and above the competitive reference product |
| **Next-best competitive alternatives (NBCA)** | Products representing the customer's best alternative if your offering didn't exist |
| **Monetary value** | Tangible cost savings or income enhancements from a product |
| **Psychological value** | Intangible satisfaction, security, prestige, or brand value |
| **Conjoint analysis** | Technique decomposing products into feature groups to discern hidden customer values |
| **Value-based segmentation** | Dividing markets into subgroups with common value drivers |
| **Metrics** | Basis for tracking value customers receive and how they pay for it |
| **Fences** | Policies, rules, programs, and structures qualifying customers for discounts or rewards |
| **Customer Value Modeling (CVM)** | Flawed approach assuming single linear relationship between price and quality |
| **Willingness-to-pay** | Maximum price a customer would pay based on perceived value |
| **Value proposition** | Statement of benefits and value delivered to customers |
| **Strategic value management** | Alternative term for strategic pricing emphasizing value focus |

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Base pricing on economic value**, not use value or cost-plus formulas
2. **Identify the next-best competitive alternative** by asking: "If I did not exist, what would my customer do?"
3. **Measure competitive prices** in units and terms familiar to customers
4. **Normalize secondary price data** for volume and service level differences to reveal consistent patterns
5. **Conduct in-depth customer interviews** to understand how your product affects customer profitability and business models
6. **Develop value driver algorithms** as simple formulas approximating how value is created
7. **Segment customers by business model** to understand differential value perception of the same features
8. **Consider charging separately** for features not all customer segments value equally
9. **Account for negative differentiation** by subtracting competitor advantages from total value delivered
10. **Use conjoint analysis** to quantify psychological value by testing customer preferences across feature sets
