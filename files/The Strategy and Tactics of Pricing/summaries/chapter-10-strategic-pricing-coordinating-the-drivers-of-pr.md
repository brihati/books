# Chapter 1: Strategic Pricing—Coordinating the Drivers of Profitability

## Detailed Chapter Summary

Strategic pricing is the coordination of independent activities to achieve sustainable profitability. Unlike the traditional view that product, promotion, and placement create value while pricing merely captures it, this chapter argues that **effective pricing is the harvest of business success**—without it, superior products and marketing efforts cannot translate into financial success.

### The Case for Strategic Pricing Over Cost-Plus Approaches

The chapter opens with research from Deloitte Consulting LLP analyzing 394 companies from 1970 to 2013, finding that focus on profitability rather than revenue growth offers a surer path to exceptional performance. Exceptional performers spend more on R&D and SG&A as a percentage of sales, building profitability through higher margins per sale rather than volume alone. This contradicts the traditional assumption that market share correlates with profitability.

The fundamental flaw in cost-plus pricing is that it reverses the proper sequence of strategic decisions. Cost-plus pricing asks: "What prices do we need to cover our costs?" Strategic pricing asks: "What costs can we afford to incur, given the prices achievable in the market?" This distinction is critical because unit costs change with volume due to fixed costs. When cost-plus pricing leads to higher prices in weak markets, sales decline, raising average unit costs further—creating a "death spiral" that is "exactly the opposite direction of a prudent strategy."

### Three Flawed Pricing Approaches

The chapter critiques three common but flawed approaches:

1. **Cost-Plus Pricing**: Assumes fixed sales volume and that price won't affect volume—an absurd assumption. It leads to overpricing in weak markets and underpricing in strong ones.

2. **Customer-Driven Pricing**: Based on what customers say they're willing to pay. This is problematic because sophisticated buyers conceal true value and have incentive to mislead when they learn prices are flexible. For innovative products, customers lack experience to judge value, so asking willingness-to-pay is ineffective.

3. **Share-Driven Pricing**: Letting competitive conditions dictate pricing rather than strategic decision-making. Price cuts offer only short-term advantage and are easily matched by competitors. Product differentiation, advertising, and distribution are more sustainable competitive tools.

### Value-Based Pricing and the Ford Mustang Example

The chapter illustrates value-based pricing through the Ford Mustang case study. Unlike traditional approaches where finance and engineering drove decisions, Ford's leading manager was a marketer. The company began by researching customer desires and found a large market of people who wanted sports-car excitement (styling, bucket seats) but couldn't afford performance-focused vehicles. Ford set a target price of under $2,500, then designed the Mustang using existing Falcon economy car mechanics with sports-car styling. Introduced in April 1964 at $2,368, it outsold any other Ford car in its first year, generating $1.1 billion in net profits in the first two years.

This approach reverses traditional product development: **target price based on estimated value drives cost decisions**, not the other way around. Value-based pricing is now standard practice at successful companies from Marriott to Boeing.

### Innovative Pricing Models Across Industries

The chapter documents how innovative pricing models have driven success:

- **Telecommunications**: Replaced price-per-minute models with monthly bundles of peak minutes plus free off-peak time, then family plans
- **Music**: Apple achieved market leadership by pricing music by the song rather than by the album; Spotify and Apple Music subscription-based streaming services are overtaking digital music store sales
- **Digital Advertising**: Online media created cost-per-click pricing metrics
- **Congestion Pricing**: London and Singapore charge fees to drive cars into congested areas during peak times; Atlanta and Minneapolis highways have express lanes with wirelessly adjusted prices during rush hours
- **Online Retail**: Retailers charge online shoppers different prices based on device type used to access the site

### Price Elasticity and Breakeven Analysis

Rather than attempting precise demand curve optimization (which is impractical due to unstable, context-dependent elasticity), the chapter recommends using **breakeven sales curves**. A product with 30 percent contribution margin could lose up to 25 percent of its volume following a 10 percent price increase before resulting in lower profitability. The practical question is: "What minimum elasticity is necessary to justify a particular price change?" rather than trying to measure actual price elasticity precisely.

Demand curves are rarely stable because price sensitivity depends on ever-changing purchase contexts and perceptions. Behavioral economics shows that how prices are presented and surrounding context significantly affect buyer responses. For example, demand for premium gasoline falls much more than demand for regular when prices rise, but demand for premium does not recover quickly when prices decline.

### The Value Cascade Framework

Strategic pricing is not a discipline separate from marketing strategy; it is a set of principles for creating marketing strategies that drive growth profitably. Setting a price level is just one step in a multistep process that impacts the full range of marketing decisions. The **Value Cascade** shows that pricing is one step in a process affecting all marketing decisions, from product features to value communication to revenue collection methods.

### Three Core Principles of Strategic Pricing

Nearly all successful pricing strategies embody three principles:

1. **Value-Based**: Differences in pricing should reflect differences in value to customers, not just market demand changes
2. **Proactive**: Companies should anticipate disruptive events and develop strategies in advance rather than react to competitor or customer terms
3. **Profit-Driven**: Success should be evaluated by earnings relative to alternative investments, not by market share or growth relative to competitors

Apple exemplifies this approach. When launching the iPhone at $499 with less-featured competitors costing half as much, Apple cut the price to $399, making it seem like a bargain. In China, rather than cutting prices to penetrate lower-income markets, Apple added functionality and built stores for exceptional customer experience. Despite Android overtaking Apple in unit sales, Apple remains the most profitable company with the highest margins.

### Price and Value Communication

Value messaging must be tailored to product type and purchase stage:

- **Search goods** (frequently purchased items like laundry detergent): Focus on specific differentiation points to help customers compare alternatives
- **Experience goods** (complex offerings like services or vacations): Deemphasize specific differentiation in favor of assurances about value delivery

Samsung adapts Galaxy S phone messaging based on whether the customer is a new user or technophile, and based on purchase stage. During information search, marketing makes differentiated features salient; during fulfillment, messaging shifts to price framing. Cellular providers describe prices in pennies per day rather than flat fees to reduce price sensitivity.

### Price Structures and Segmentation

Price structures reflect differences in value created or ability to pay across customer segments. Complex price structures allow firms to maximize revenue from different customer types willing to pay different amounts. Airlines exemplify this: a Boston to Los Angeles roundtrip ranges from $324 (non-refundable) to $1,901 (fully flexible first-class). Ryanair rose to first place among European airlines in less than a decade despite extreme price structure complexity, charging €5 for airport check-in, €10 per checked bag, €20 for a baby on lap, and €3 to board near the front of line.

### Three Pillars of Strategic Pricing Implementation

A successful pricing strategy requires three pillars:

1. **Effective Organization**: Clear decision rights prevent pricing from becoming a political exercise. Without clearly specified authority, pricing decisions become power struggles between functional areas.

2. **Timely and Accurate Information**: Pricing decision-makers require quality information from multiple sources: customer value data, competitive pricing, cost data, and volume data. Many companies adopt price management systems integrated with data warehouses to distribute the right information to the right managers.

3. **Appropriately Motivated Management**: Incentive structures often undermine pricing strategy by rewarding volume over profitability. Sales representatives trained to sell on value but paid solely on volume maximization creates conflicting incentives. Ranking sales reps by price realization can motivate more profitable behaviors.

### The Alamo Rent A Car Cautionary Tale

The chapter illustrates the importance of anticipating competitive reactions through the Alamo case study. In the early 1990s, Alamo was the most profitable rental car company despite being fifth-largest, dominating leisure rentals. Management pursued growth in business travel by undercutting Hertz and Avis at airports. Hertz responded by opening the largest car rental facility in the world in Orlando, Florida with 66 counters, undercutting Alamo's deals with European tour operators. Within two years, Alamo's profits fell into the red and operations were sold. The lesson: firms with large market share must anticipate and manage competitive reactions.

### Price Determination: Reference Value and Differentiating Value

In most markets, the largest portion of price is determined by competition, not value-in-use to customers. Market value comprises:

- **Reference Value**: Value that is the same as that offered by competitive alternatives
- **Differentiating Value**: Value that differentiates a product from competitive alternatives

Managing price competition involves influencing reference value. Companies that adopted a value-based pricing strategy and built organizational capabilities to implement it earned **24 percent higher profits** than industry peers. However, 23 percent of marketing and sales managers did not understand their company's pricing strategy.

## Key Points

- Marketing has four elements: product, promotion, placement, and price. The first three create value; pricing captures it.
- Ineffective pricing can prevent successful product, promotion, and placement efforts from resulting in financial success.
- Profit margins should be prioritized over growth; how a company invests to grow is a better predictor of financial success than market share alone.
- Strategic pricing requires anticipating price levels before product development begins.
- Cost-plus pricing is fundamentally flawed because unit costs change with volume due to fixed costs.
- Value-based pricing lets anticipated pricing determine costs incurred rather than the reverse.
- Strategic pricing aims to capture more value, not necessarily make more sales.
- Price has little impact on whether customers try innovative products.
- Most profitable price sometimes substantially restricts market share relative to competition.
- Strategic pricing requires informed trade-offs between price and volume to maximize long-term profitability.
- Demand curves are rarely stable or easily measured because sensitivity to price depends on ever-changing purchase contexts and perceptions.
- Breakeven sales curves are more practical than attempting precise demand curve optimization.
- Strategic pricing is the coordination of otherwise independent activities to achieve sustainable profitability.
- Demand for most products is not given but created by seller decisions about offerings, communication, pricing, and customer expectations.
- Nearly all successful pricing strategies embody three principles: value-based, proactive, and profit-driven.
- Price perception depends on entire purchase situation, not just rational value calculation.
- Price structures reflect differences in value created or ability to pay across customer segments.
- Success of pricing strategy depends on customers being willing to pay the charged price.
- In most markets, the largest portion of price is determined by competition, not value-in-use to customers.
- Companies that adopted value-based pricing earned 24 percent higher profits than industry peers.
- A successful pricing strategy requires three pillars: effective organization, timely information, and appropriately motivated management.

## Important Examples and Stories

### Ford Mustang (1964)
Unlike traditional approaches where finance and engineering drove decisions, Ford put a marketer in charge. The company researched customer desires and found demand for affordable sports-car excitement. Ford set a target price of under $2,500, then designed the Mustang using existing Falcon mechanics with sports-car styling. Introduced April 1964 at $2,368 base price, it outsold any other Ford car in its first year with $1.1 billion net profits in the first two years. From an engineering perspective it was not the most technically advanced, but from customers' perspective it represented better value than anything else in the market.

### Apple iPhone Pricing Strategy
Apple launched the iPhone at $499 with less-featured competitors costing half as much. A price cut to $399 made it seem like a bargain. In China, rather than cutting prices to penetrate lower-income markets, Apple added functionality and built stores for exceptional customer experience. Despite Android overtaking Apple in unit sales, Apple remains the most profitable company with the highest margins.

### Alamo Rent A Car (Early 1990s)
Alamo was the most profitable rental car company despite being fifth-largest, dominating leisure rentals. Management pursued growth in business travel by undercutting Hertz and Avis at airports. Hertz responded by opening the largest car rental facility in the world in Orlando, Florida with 66 counters, undercutting Alamo's deals with European tour operators. Within two years, Alamo's profits fell into the red and operations were sold.

### Cable TV Pricing Spiral
Cable TV companies offered heavily-discounted first-year contracts ($99/month) to build loyalty, but widespread advertising of the deals taught customers to threaten switching at renewal. Sales reps were forced to waive increases, which then incentivized even more customers to threaten switching—a negative feedback loop that eroded pricing power.

### Ryanair's Price Structure Complexity
Ryanair rose to first place among European airlines in less than a decade despite extreme price structure complexity. The airline charges €5 for airport check-in, €10 per checked bag, €20 for a baby on lap, €20 each for car seat and stroller, and €3 to board near the front of line. By removing 2 of 3 toilets on a 737, Ryanair added 6 extra seats.

### Airline Pricing Example
A Boston to Los Angeles roundtrip ranges from $324 (non-refundable ticket with $100 change fee and $15 checked baggage each way) to $514 (refundable/changeable ticket) to $934 (first-class non-cancellable) to $1,901 (fully flexible first-class).

### Gasoline Price Elasticity
When gasoline prices rise, demand for premium grades falls much more than demand for regular grades, but demand for premium does not recover quickly when prices decline.

### Wine Price Perception
A $30 bottle is perceived as a bargain when surrounded by higher prices but expensive when surrounded by $20 alternatives.

### Samsung Galaxy S Messaging
Samsung adapts Galaxy S phone messaging based on whether the customer is a new user or technophile, and based on purchase stage. During information search, marketing focuses on big screens and high data-transfer speeds; during fulfillment, messaging shifts to price framing.

### Noosa International Rainy Weather Rebate
Noosa International offered a "Rainy Weather Rebate" of 20 percent discount on hotel accommodations if it rained during a customer's vacation.

### Sales Manager Price Realization Ranking
A senior salesperson promoted to regional sales manager in an area with rampant discounting ranked sales reps by price realization and invited top performers to describe profitable deals and bottom performers to discuss challenges, repeating this quarterly. One month later, sales reps were asking to see their rankings, showing high motivation to engage in productive behaviors.

## Definitions and Terms

- **Strategic Pricing**: The coordination of otherwise independent activities to achieve sustainable profitability.
- **Value-Based Pricing**: Pricing that begins before investments are made and lets anticipated pricing determine costs incurred rather than the reverse; differences in pricing should reflect differences in value to customers.
- **Cost-Plus Pricing**: A pricing procedure that aims to yield a fair return over all costs, fully and fairly allocated; fundamentally flawed because unit costs change with volume.
- **Economic Value Estimation (EVE®)**: A concept for determining the economic value of a product or service based on the alternatives customers have available to satisfy the same need.
- **Proactive Pricing**: Companies anticipate disruptive events and develop strategies in advance to deal with them.
- **Profit-Driven Pricing**: Companies evaluate success at price management by what they earn relative to alternative investments rather than by market share and growth.
- **Breakeven Sales Change**: The answer to "what percent change in sales would be necessary for a proposed price change to maintain the same total profit contribution."
- **Breakeven Sales Curve**: A graph of breakeven sales changes associated with different potential price changes, representing how much demand is needed to maintain current profitability as prices change.
- **Price Elasticity of Demand**: The actual percentage change in sales divided by the percentage change in price.
- **Reference Value**: Value that is the same as that offered by competitive alternatives.
- **Differentiating Value**: Value that differentiates a product from competitive alternatives.
- **Pricing Policy**: Rules or habits, either explicit or cultural, that determine how a company varies its prices when faced with factors other than value and cost to serve.
- **Price Realization**: The actual price achieved on deals relative to list or target price.
- **Search Goods**: Frequently purchased products like laundry detergent or personal care items.
- **Experience Goods**: Complex products such as services or vacations.
- **Penetration Pricing**: Pricing strategy to drive volume and gain market share.
- **Yield Management**: The projection process used for airline pricing.
- **PIMS Database**: Originally stood for Profit Impact of Market Share; later redefined to stand for Profit Impact of Marketing Strategy.
- **Value Cascade**: A framework showing that pricing is one step in a multistep process affecting all marketing decisions.
