# CHAPTER 9: From Hoping to Knowing

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter demonstrates how to build an **outside-in business case** for new products by incorporating external customer data on willingness to pay (WTP), moving from internal assumptions to data-driven decision making. The chapter uses Manheim's DealShield Purchase Guarantee as a primary case study and a software-as-a-service freemium model as a cautionary example.

### The Problem with Current Business Cases

Approximately **95 percent of business cases are built from the inside out**, failing to account for critical market information. Only about **5 percent of companies have essential WTP information in their business cases**. Without knowing what customers will actually pay and how demand changes with price, companies cannot trust their business cases—they are merely guessing about monetization potential.

### The Manheim DealShield Case Study

In 2011, Manheim leadership debated whether to launch a no-questions-asked vehicle return service. Some executives believed in the idea based on gut feeling and wanted to move quickly; others questioned viability due to risk concerns. Rather than experiencing decision paralysis, **Manheim commissioned a value research study in 2012** and conducted WTP talks with customers before full productization.

The resulting **DealShield Purchase Guarantee** allows dealers to return vehicles purchased at Manheim auctions for any reason within **21 days and 500 miles**. The service debuted in 2012 and by 2015 had protected billions of dollars in vehicle purchases and generated significant revenue and profit.

Research revealed that **WTP varied significantly by customer segment**: price-sensitive dealers were willing to pay only for short-term guarantees, while risk-averse dealers were willing to pay higher prices for longer return periods. WTP also varied based on car type and condition, as defect rates differ substantially by brand, model, and vehicle condition.

### The Four Pillars Framework

A robust business case must integrate **four critical elements**: price, value, volume, and cost. These elements are interdependent—changes to one affect the others. For example:
- Raising prices almost always lowers volume and thus revenue
- Lower prices typically increase volume
- Manufacturing costs per unit will increase if a key supplier fails and you must source from higher-priced suppliers

**Price elasticity**—how demand changes at different price points—is a critical element often avoided by companies but essential for accurate business cases.

### The Freemium Model Cautionary Tale

A software-as-a-service provider planned a freemium model with zero base service price to drive sign-ups, hoping customers would upgrade to premium. Analysis revealed it would **cannibalize their existing low-priced entry products**, resulting in net revenue loss. Executives abandoned the concept after seeing the detailed business case.

### Living, Breathing Documents

Business case documents should be **living documents that evolve as product definition progresses** through customer segments, configurations, bundles, monetization models, and pricing strategy. They should not be static documents created once for funding approval and then shelved. Business cases must be checked and updated at each decision point and throughout the product lifecycle with new data on pricing, costs, volume, and value.

### Nine Steps to Build a Robust Business Case

The chapter outlines nine critical steps (referenced but detailed in the source material) that emphasize:
1. Gathering data-verified facts to avoid overstating target market size and creating unrealistic adoption assumptions
2. Attaching risk assumptions to inherently uncertain input parameters, with best- and worst-case outcomes calculated
3. Using Monte Carlo simulations (such as Oracle's Crystal Ball) to model uncertainty using probabilistic distributions
4. Prioritizing goals through 'what if' scenarios to understand trade-offs between profit, revenue, volume, and margin
5. Modeling competitive reactions and quantifying their impact
6. Assessing the new product's overall impact on the company, including cannibalization of existing products
7. Preserving linkages among the four pillars when changes are made
8. Determining customer WTP before finalizing the business case
9. Including price elasticity analysis to understand how volume changes with price adjustments

### Why This Matters

A strong business case helps defy the **72 percent new-product failure rate** and helps avoid monetizing innovation failure types including undead products, minivations, feature shock, and hidden gems. Without customer WTP and price elasticity data, a business case will only tell you what you want to hear.

## Definitions and Terms

- **Willingness to Pay (WTP)**: Customers' maximum price they are willing to pay for a product or service
- **Price Elasticity**: How demand changes at different price points; the dynamic relationship where sales volume tends to go down when product price goes up
- **DealShield Purchase Guarantee**: A service allowing dealers to return vehicles purchased at Manheim auctions for any reason within 21 days and 500 miles
- **Outside-in Business Case**: A business case built from external customer data and market information, rather than internal assumptions
- **Living Document**: A business case that is updated before and after launch with new data on pricing, costs, volume, and value
- **Cannibalization**: When a new product reduces sales of existing products
- **Price Elasticity Curve**: How demand changes at different price points
- **Monetizing Innovation**: The process of creating revenue and profit from new products
- **Undead Products**: Products that fail to gain traction but continue to consume resources
- **Minivation**: A minor innovation with limited market impact
- **Feature Shock**: Overwhelming customers with too many features they don't value
- **Hidden Gem**: A successful product that was underestimated or overlooked
- **Monte Carlo Simulations**: Probabilistic modeling techniques (such as Oracle's Crystal Ball) to model uncertainty

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Gather WTP data early**: Determine customer willingness to pay before finalizing your business case using methods from Chapter 4. Commission value research studies and conduct early WTP talks with customers to understand what factors impact their willingness to pay.

2. **Create a living business case**: Create your first business case version immediately after determining high-level WTP for your product. Continuously evolve the business case as you progress through defining customer segments, product configurations, monetization models, and pricing strategies. Update it throughout the product lifecycle with new data.

3. **Integrate the four pillars**: Model the linkages among price, value, volume, and cost to create more precise monetary forecasts. Keep checking the business case at each decision point to ensure the four pillars remain integrated, consistent, and aligned with company strategy.

4. **Include price elasticity analysis**: Understand how volume changes with price adjustments. Know in advance what would happen if you increased your price by 20 percent.

5. **Use data-verified facts**: Apply data-verified facts based on real figures to avoid overstating market size and adoption assumptions. Develop business cases on undeniable facts from customer feedback rather than internal assumptions and opinions.

6. **Assess WTP by segment**: Assess how WTP varies by customer type, product characteristics, and conditions to inform segmented pricing strategies. Base pricing strategy recommendations on four critical elements: business risks, customer value, customer WTP, and price elasticity.

7. **Add risk assumptions**: Attach risk assumptions to input parameters that are inherently uncertain and calculate best- and worst-case outcomes. Use statistically significant survey data to simulate demand across different price points and scenarios.

8. **Use probabilistic modeling**: Use Monte Carlo simulations (such as Oracle's Crystal Ball) to model uncertainty using probabilistic distributions.

9. **Model competitive reactions**: Model competitive reactions and quantify their impact in your business case.

10. **Assess overall company impact**: Assess the new product's overall impact on the company, including cannibalization of existing products.

11. **Test before committing**: Test new offerings in the market with customer research before committing significant funds to full productization. Finding out early if there is minimal WTP prevents sinking resources into doomed products.

12. **Prioritize features based on WTP**: Prioritize features based on customer willingness to pay for them to prevent feature shock.
