# CHAPTER 8: Price Low for Market Share or High for Premium Branding?

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### Foundations of Pricing Strategy

A pricing strategy is a short- and long-term monetization plan that requires three essential elements: clear intent, quantifiable goals, and a defined time frame. The chapter emphasizes that "the word 'because' is the most important word in any pricing strategy" as it forces thorough thinking and enables team buy-in. Best-performing companies treat pricing strategy as a "living and breathing document" that is continually updated rather than shelved after creation.

The fundamental challenge in pricing is that different goals—revenue, market share, total profit, profit margin, and customer lifetime value—cannot be maximized simultaneously. Companies must make explicit trade-offs. For example, a product priced at $10 yields 100 customers with 30% margin, while pricing at $15 yields 80 customers with greater than 50% margin. This illustrates the core tension: accepting 20% fewer customers in return for 20% increased revenue requires deliberate choice.

A Goal Allocation Exercise helps executives prioritize competing objectives by allocating 100 points across goals, forcing alignment on which objectives matter most.

### Three Pricing Strategy Types

The chapter identifies three distinct pricing strategies:

**Maximization Strategy** determines the optimal price at which profit or revenue reaches maximum on the price elasticity curve. This approach maximizes short-term profit or revenue by finding the single best price point.

**Penetration Strategy** (also called "land-and-expand") intentionally prices products lower than maximization strategy would suggest to rapidly gain market share. This strategy is appropriate in markets dominated by network effects or high customer loyalty to the first brand. However, it is the riskiest from profit and revenue standpoints. Successful examples include:
- **Facebook**: Started as a free service that accumulated hundreds of millions of users before monetizing with ads. By 2014 with 1 billion members, advertisers spent $12 billion annually on Facebook ads.
- **Amazon and Uber**: Used penetration strategy with low pricing to create barriers to entry.
- **Ariba**: Initially free for sellers, later monetized seller offerings.
- **Cloud software companies**: Used penetration after freemium models.

The cautionary tale of **LivingSocial** demonstrates penetration strategy failure: the company raised $900 million in venture capital but accumulated losses exceeding $1 billion by 2015 because "no one paid much attention to how the company would ultimately make money." Penetration strategy requires either the ability to expand revenue from customers or the capacity to gain huge market share rapidly.

**Skimming Strategy** first caters to customers with higher willingness to pay (early adopters), then systematically decreases price to reach segments with lower willingness to pay. This strategy is appropriate when customers' willingness to pay varies greatly between early adopters and late followers. **Porsche** exemplified this by launching the eight-cylinder Panamera first, then releasing a six-cylinder model a year later. **Samsung** and **Microsoft's Xbox** also employed skimming strategies. Skimming requires higher initial pricing than maximization strategy would suggest.

### Price-Setting Principles and Operational Components

Senior executives often don't understand connections among price, volume, and profit. Price-setting principles must be established early to keep pricing strategy on track and avoid knee-jerk reactions. The chapter identifies five operational components:

1. **Monetization models**: Whether the model is the same across all customer segments and whether it changes over the product lifecycle
2. **Price differentiation**: Whether prices will differ and on what basis
3. **Price floors**: Minimum prices below which the company will never price
4. **Price endings**: Common B2C price endings are $30, $29.50, $29.99, or $29.95; whole numbers typically work better in B2B
5. **Price increases**: Whether prices will increase over time, and if so, how much and at what frequency

**Promotional principles** should address both customer behavior (promotional reactions) and competitor behavior (competitive reactions). **Walmart's Every Day Low Price strategy** uses low-entry pricing through promotions to establish a low-price image, while **Apple** uses a premium strategy with minimal promotions.

### Price Elasticity and Optimization

Price elasticity is the most important input for optimizing price. It is calculated as: **Price Elasticity = Change in Sales (%)/Change in Price (%)**.

The price-elasticity curve (also called the demand curve or price-demand relationship) shows how sales volume decreases and increases when price moves up or down. Critically, "every product—from a Rolls Royce to a pack of chewing gum—has a price elasticity curve," and elasticity varies greatly across products. Companies cannot use general assumptions or industry averages; they must measure or estimate the price elasticity level for their specific new product.

The shape of the price-elasticity curve is the most important input for calculating optimal price:
- **High price elasticity** (steep demand curve) results in relatively low optimal margin
- **Low price elasticity** (flat demand curve) leads to high optimal margin

For example, if a price increases by 5% and volume falls 10%, the price elasticity is 2, indicating a steep curve. The steeper the price-elasticity curve, the more sales volume is lost when raising prices.

**Practical example**: A product launch scenario shows that at $100, 1 million units sell; at $70, 1.35 million units; at $130, 600,000 units. With $50 variable cost per unit and $25 million fixed cost, the optimal profit price is $110, accepting 10% lower volume than the $100 price point.

### Competitive Reaction Planning

War-gaming sessions should be conducted prior to launch to anticipate competitor moves and prepare counterreactions. Planning reactions to market conditions is "like playing chess and thinking a few moves ahead." Companies that don't think ahead react spontaneously and make unintentional yet avoidable mistakes.

Key questions to address:
- How might competitors react, and why?
- Are competitors likely to react only once?
- If we match a competitor's price, what will be the impact on our revenue and profit?
- What counterreactions do we expect to our reactions?

### Impact of Pricing Strategy on Monetization Potential

Companies with well-defined pricing strategies are **40 percent more likely to realize their monetizing potential** than those without one. The stakes are high: a $20 pricing error can easily cost 20% of total profit. The smaller the margin per unit, the bigger the impact of suboptimal pricing.

Warren Buffett noted that "the single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power."

## Key Points

- A pricing strategy requires clear intent, quantifiable goals, and a defined time frame
- Different pricing goals cannot be maximized simultaneously; trade-offs are mandatory
- Three strategy types exist: maximization (optimal short-term profit), penetration (rapid market share gain), and skimming (early adopter premium pricing)
- Price elasticity is unique to each product and must be measured or estimated individually
- High elasticity leads to low optimal margins; low elasticity leads to high optimal margins
- Five operational components must be defined: monetization models, price differentiation, price floors, price endings, and price increases
- Promotional and competitive reaction principles should be established before launch
- Well-defined pricing strategies increase likelihood of realizing monetization potential by 40%
- Pricing strategy should be a living document continually updated, not a static plan

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Pricing strategy** | Short- and long-term monetization plan with clear intent, quantifiable goals, and time frame |
| **Maximization strategy** | Pricing that maximizes profit or revenue in the short term by determining optimal price on price elasticity curve |
| **Penetration strategy** | Intentionally pricing lower than maximization strategy to rapidly gain market share; also called "land-and-expand" |
| **Skimming strategy** | First catering to customers with higher willingness to pay (early adopters), then systematically decreasing price to reach lower-willingness segments |
| **Price elasticity** | Ratio of percentage change in volume to percentage change in price (e.g., elasticity of 2 means 5% price increase causes 10% volume loss) |
| **Price-elasticity curve** | Shows how sales volume decreases and increases when price moves up or down; also called demand curve or price-demand relationship |
| **Willingness to pay (WTP)** | Customer's maximum price they will accept for a product |

| **Land-and-expand** | Alternative term for penetration strategy |
| **Price floor** | Minimum price below which company will never price |
| **Price differentiation** | Strategy of charging different prices based on customer segments or other factors |

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Establish clear, overriding goals before setting prices** to avoid contradictory strategies; use a Goal Allocation Exercise with C-suite executives to force trade-offs and align on priorities

2. **Document your pricing strategy simply and clearly** so it can be communicated to gain team buy-in; treat it as a living document continually updated rather than a static plan

3. **Understand that maximizing market share requires different strategies than maximizing profit margin**; accept that you cannot optimize all goals simultaneously

4. **Determine your five operational price-setting components early**: monetization models, price differentiation, price floors, price endings, and price increases

5. **Develop promotional principles early**, deciding whether to use promotions like Walmart's Every Day Low Price or minimize them like Apple

6. **Conduct war-gaming sessions before launch** to anticipate competitor reactions and prepare counterreactions; plan reactions to market conditions like "playing chess and thinking a few moves ahead"

7. **Calculate price elasticity using customer willingness-to-pay data and cost information** to find the optimal price point; remember that steeper price-elasticity curves result in more volume loss when raising prices

8. **Factor in both variable costs per unit and fixed costs** when calculating profit at different price points

9. **Choose penetration strategy only when you must gain market share quickly** in network-effect or high-loyalty markets, and ensure you can follow through on planned future price increases

10. **Use skimming strategy when customers' willingness to pay varies greatly** between early adopters and late followers; implement by launching higher-end products first, then releasing lower-priced versions

11. **Measure or estimate the price elasticity curve specific to your new product** rather than using industry averages; recognize that elasticity varies greatly across products

12. **Remember that a $20 pricing error can easily cost 20% of total profit**; the smaller the margin per unit, the bigger the impact of suboptimal pricing
