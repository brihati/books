# CHAPTER 3: Why Good People Get It Wrong

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter diagnoses why good people and companies fail at monetizing innovation despite good intentions. The core problem is a **72% failure rate** in new product initiatives, with most ending up as minivations, feature shocks, hidden gems, or undeads (failure categories from Chapter 2).

### Root Cause: Delayed Monetization Thinking

Companies typically delay monetization considerations until the last moment in product development. This practice stems from a prevailing mindset that treats monetization as "unsavory, dirty, and detrimental to true innovation." The reigning belief holds that asking product developers to think about pricing infringes on their creativity and that financial considerations encumber the innovation process.

### The Five Myths Framework

The chapter identifies that five underlying myths create a false belief system justifying the isolation of innovation teams from financial and market considerations. These myths perpetuate the "build it, and they will come" mentality—a philosophy supported by celebrated examples of rejected works that eventually succeeded.

### Celebrated Rejection Stories (Exceptions, Not Rules)

The chapter presents famous examples of initially rejected ideas that later succeeded:
- **Harry Potter** was rejected 12 times before publication
- **Star Wars** was criticized by George Lucas's friends and fellow directors when it came out
- **Catch-22** and **Gone with the Wind** were rejected by dozens of publishers
- **Vincent Van Gogh** died in poverty; his paintings now sell for tens of millions
- **Fred Smith's FedEx** business plan earned a failing grade in business school

However, the chapter emphasizes that these are exceptions: **95% of rejected ideas actually fail**. The success stories create a misleading narrative that justifies ignoring market viability during development.

### Cultural Messaging Against Monetization

The chapter cites advertising campaigns that reinforce the anti-monetization mindset:
- **Apple's 1997 "Think Different" campaign** featured Miles Davis to appeal to the innovator as artistic underdog
- **Fiat Chrysler's 2012 Dodge Dart campaign** proclaimed "Kickout the finance guys!" (from 33 initial advertising concepts)

### The New Paradigm: Early Willingness-to-Pay Assessment

The chapter proposes a fundamentally different approach: **understanding customer willingness to pay before committing resources** dramatically increases success likelihood. This involves:

1. **Designing products around a price** determined during the concept stage
2. **Assessing market viability early**, long before launch
3. **Incorporating monetization front and center** in product development rather than isolating innovation teams

The chapter argues that putting monetization first is **not antithetical to innovation**. Instead, early monetization assessment can spur product development organizations to new heights by making the innovation process far more reliable.

### Transition to Part 2

The chapter concludes by introducing **Part 2**, which presents nine counterintuitive rules for monetizing innovation that differ from traditional product development approaches taught in business schools and learned through career experience.

## Key Points

- 72% failure rate of innovation initiatives demonstrates the cost of delayed monetization thinking
- Companies isolate innovation teams from financial and market considerations based on five myths
- The "build it, and they will come" mindset is supported by celebrated exceptions (rejected works that succeeded), not the rule
- 95% of rejected ideas actually fail, contradicting the narrative of famous rejections
- Designing products around a price point increases chances of survival and thriving
- Understanding customer willingness to pay at the concept stage makes innovation more reliable
- Early monetization assessment eliminates uncertainty about market viability before launch
- Monetization front and center is compatible with—not detrimental to—true innovation

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Understand customer willingness to pay before committing resources** — Assess whether customers will actually pay for your invention before investing heavily in development and launch

2. **Design products around a price point** — Determine the target price during the concept stage and design features accordingly to increase survival and thriving likelihood

3. **Assess market viability early** — Figure out how much customers will pay when the product is still in concept stage to make the innovation process more reliable

4. **Include market data and financial considerations from the start** — Rather than isolating innovation teams, integrate customers' perspectives and financial realities into product development

5. **Know your product will succeed before launch** — By designing with features customers get excited about and embracing a specific price point, you gain confidence in marketplace success

6. **Reject the false choice between creativity and monetization** — Recognize that putting monetization front and center can spur product development organizations to new heights, not constrain them
