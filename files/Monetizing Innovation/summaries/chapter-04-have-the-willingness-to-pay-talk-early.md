# CHAPTER 4: Have the "Willingness-to-Pay" Talk Early

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Case for Early WTP Conversations

Pricing discussions must occur at the beginning of product development, not at the end. Willingness-to-pay (WTP)—an indication of what customers value and how much they are willing to pay for that value—should inform product design and feature prioritization from the outset. Early WTP conversations help determine if a monetizable market opportunity exists, prevent costly feature overload, and enable companies to shape their own destiny rather than hoping customers will pay.

A 2014 survey of 1,600 companies across 40 countries found that 80% delay pricing discussions until just before product introduction. Consequently, 72% of new products fail to meet revenue or profit targets. This approach leaves companies operating "on the huge hope that customers will be willing to pay for their products and services, but in reality they simply don't know."

### The Gillette Guard Case Study

Gillette's successful launch of the Guard razor in India demonstrates the power of early WTP conversations. In 2009, Gillette held only 22% market share in India because the Mach 3, priced at 100 rupees ($2.24), was too expensive for most consumers. India's potential shaving market was estimated at 4 times the size of America's, with 400 million men compared to 94 million in the United States.

P&G spent thousands of hours interviewing Indian consumers to distinguish must-have from nice-to-have features. Through these conversations, they determined a target price of 15 rupees for the razor and 5 rupees for replacement blades. The Guard was engineered around this price point with only 4 components instead of the Mach 3's 25, including a hollow handle. This laser-like focus on essential features allowed the product to be offered at an affordable price. By 2012, the Guard captured over 60% of the razor category in India—nearly three times Gillette's previous share.

As one executive noted: "The first job is to bring more consumers into Gillette. When they start enjoying a better shave, they'll be more open to all solutions."

### Feature Validation and the Feature Shock Problem

An innovation team planning an internet marketplace service identified 25 features through brainstorming but could not prove customers would value them. After conducting WTP validation with several hundred potential customers, they discovered customers were willing to pay $10–$20 monthly but valued only 10 of the 25 features. Notably, the Facebook connection feature—which the team believed would be critical—ranked only seventh in customer preference.

This WTP conversation allowed the company to prioritize features and create a product roadmap based on actual customer value, not internal assumptions. The team avoided "feature shock"—overloading products with unnecessary features that force high prices and reduce demand.

A parallel example involved an internet company hosting web pages and registering domain names. After nearly a year of insufficient revenue with a 27-feature product, they reduced it to 8 features and increased the price. This change boosted sales conversion rate and revenue more than 25%, demonstrating that fewer features can create more demand by highlighting what truly matters to customers.

### Four Types of Product Failures Prevented by Early WTP

Early WTP conversations help avoid:
1. **Feature shock**: Products overloaded with unnecessary features forcing high prices
2. **Minivations**: Products priced too low due to lack of customer value information
3. **Undead products**: Products that fail to generate sufficient revenue
4. **Inside-out thinking**: Designing products based on internal capabilities rather than customer needs

### Ten Key Insights for Conducting WTP Conversations

**1. Conduct Expert Judgment Workshops First**
Form internal cross-functional teams from product, sales, and marketing to pilot-test WTP questions before customer conversations.

**2. Frame as "Value Talks," Not "Pricing Discussions"**
Positioning discussions as value talks gets customers in the right mindset. Example opening: "We want to talk about our latest innovation ideas and how we can continue to add value for you."

**3. Use Direct Questions**
Simple, direct questions often yield important insights:
- "Do you value these products/features?"
- "What would you consider an acceptable price?"
- "Why do you say that?"

**4. Make 25% of Questions Open-Ended "Why" Questions**
Follow pricing questions with "Why?" to understand customer reasoning and uncover deeper insights about decision-making.

**5. Mix Structured with Unstructured Conversations**
Combine structured scripts with unstructured dialogue. Expect key insights to come from the simplest questions.

**6. Involve Cross-Functional Teams Directly**
Have key people from product, sales, and marketing attend focus groups and interviews to hear customer voices directly, improving chances of monetizing innovation.

**7. Analyze Distribution, Not Averages**
When two customer groups show willingness to pay $20 and $100 respectively, the average of $60 is misleading. Better to create two versions at $20 and $100 to serve both segments. Relying on averages can leave money on the table or make products unaffordable to segments.

**8. Conduct Qualitative Before Quantitative**
Perform one-on-one or focus group discussions before launching quantitative surveys. Qualitative insights create more robust surveys and provide sanity checks.

**9. Use Precise Language**
Questions "Would you buy this?" and "Would you buy this for $20?" are totally different and yield different responses. Precision matters.

**10. Keep Design Simple**
Avoid overwhelming customers with too many or overly complicated questions.

### Five Types of Research Questions

1. **Direct questions**: "What would you consider an acceptable price?"
2. **Purchase probability**: Assessing likelihood of purchase at various price points
3. **Most-least questions**: Identifying which features customers value most and least
4. **Build-your-own questions**: Showing product lineups with different price points and feature combinations, asking which customers would choose
5. **Purchase simulations**: Scenario-based testing to understand how customers make choices between feature and price combinations

### CEO-Level Assessment Questions

- Does our product development team have serious pricing discussions with customers in the early stages of new product development?
- What data do we have to show there's a viable market that can and will pay for our new product?
- Do we know our market's WTP range for our product concept and what price range is considered acceptable or expensive?
- Do we know what features customers truly value and are willing to pay for, and which ones they don't?
- What are our product's differentiating features versus competitors' features and how much do customers value them?

## Definitions and Terms

- **Willingness to Pay (WTP)**: An indication of what customers value and a measure of how much they are willing to pay for that value; the price range customers have in mind and their willingness to pay for each feature
- **Feature Shock**: When a product has too many features that customers don't care about, which reduces demand and willingness to pay
- **Minivations**: Products priced too low due to lack of customer value information
- **Undead Product**: A product that fails to generate sufficient revenue
- **Inside-Out Thinking**: Designing products based on internal capabilities rather than customer needs
- **Value Talk**: Positioning customer discussions about pricing as conversations about value rather than pricing
- **Mental Models**: Customer decision-making frameworks and reasoning
- **Purchase Scenario Simulation**: Testing how customers make choices between feature and price combinations

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Conduct WTP discussions with potential customers before beginning product design and development**, not after the product is built
2. **Use customer interviews and observations** in homes and shopping trips to identify must-have vs. nice-to-have features
3. **Design products around a target price** determined by customer willingness to pay
4. **Validate that customers see value** in your product concept and are willing to pay for it before investing in development
5. **Use WTP validation to prove market existence** for product ideas to secure firm investment and resources
6. **Form internal cross-functional expert groups** and conduct pilot tests of WTP questions before customer conversations
7. **Start customer discussions by asking about pain points** and product value before transitioning to pricing questions
8. **Make approximately 25% of questions open-ended "why" questions** to uncover deeper insights
9. **Have key people from product, sales, and marketing attend** focus groups and interviews to hear customer voices directly
10. **Analyze the distribution of WTP responses** rather than relying on average prices to identify market segments
11. **Conduct qualitative one-on-one or focus group discussions** before launching quantitative surveys
12. **Use precise language in questions** to ensure customers answer your actual underlying question
13. **Keep survey design simple** and avoid overwhelming customers with too many or overly complicated questions
14. **Position pricing discussions as "value talks"** to encourage honest feedback
15. **Identify which features customers truly value** and are willing to pay for, then kill or add features based on WTP data
16. **Compare your product's differentiating features** against competitors and measure customer valuation of the difference
17. **Consider reducing features and increasing price** if product has feature shock
18. **Investigate customer mental models** and decision-making rules through iterative scenario testing
