# CHAPTER 7: Go Beyond the Price Point

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Primacy of Monetization Models

Chapter 7 establishes that **how you charge customers is as important as—or more important than—what you charge or the price point itself**. A monetization model defines how customers pay for a product or service, and selecting the right model can be as transformative as the innovation itself. Most companies perpetuate existing monetization models without strategic reflection or testing, missing opportunities to differentiate and capture value.

The chapter argues that "a bad monetization model can be worse than a bad price" and that "increasingly the monetization model is the innovation." Innovative monetization models have driven game-changing results across hosting, cloud computing, telecommunications, transportation, and aircraft engines—often benefiting established companies as much as newer entrants.

### The Michelin Case Study: From Tires to Ton-Kilometers

Michelin's transformation illustrates the power of monetization model innovation. In the early 2000s, Michelin faced premium price pressure from Chinese and South Korean tire manufacturers. The company had invented longer-lasting tires (lasting 20% longer than competitors), but realized that even a small price increase would cause a 20% drop in customer demand.

Rather than raising prices or increasing sales and marketing spend, Michelin revolutionized its monetization model. In 2001, Michelin's Fleet Solutions introduced the **ton-kilometer (TK) model**, charging trucking fleets by mileage driven rather than by number of tires purchased. This shift allowed Michelin to:
- Fully capitalize on the value of longer-lasting tires without requiring additional sales and marketing
- Align customer payments with actual product performance and value delivered
- Bear the risk of tire failure, incentivizing quality

By 2011, Michelin's earnings before interest and taxes were **25% higher than Bridgestone's and more than three times Goodyear's**—the highest profits in the tire industry. The company had transformed a commodity business into a performance-based partnership.

### Five Core Monetization Models

The chapter presents five proven monetization models applicable across industries:

#### 1. **Subscription Model**
Customers provide periodic and automatic payment for continued delivery of or access to a company's offering. Subscription models:
- Lock out competitors whose goods are only available on a per-transaction basis
- Increase lifetime customer revenue and create recurring revenue streams
- Reduce customer churn and enable cross-selling and upselling
- Make expensive products more affordable through bite-size payment chunks

**Examples:**
- Netflix (founded 1999): flat monthly subscription fee for unlimited movie rentals by mail, later displacing Blockbuster. By 2012, Netflix had 33 million streaming subscribers; by Q3 2015, 69 million.
- Spotify and Pandora: subscription plans for online music services
- Dollar Shave Club: subscription model for shaving supplies
- Wall Street Journal and New York Times: subscription models for newspaper delivery
- Music streaming subscriptions grew six-fold since 2009, generating $1.6 billion in 2014 (nearly a quarter of global music industry's digital revenue)

#### 2. **Dynamic Pricing Model**
Prices fluctuate based on factors such as season, time of day, weather conditions, demand, and supply. Dynamic pricing boosts monetization of fixed and constrained capacity by adjusting prices to match demand.

**Examples:**
- Uber (launched 2010): dynamic pricing tied to real-time demand and supply of taxis in a given location
- Professional and collegiate sports: higher and lower-profile games priced accordingly
- Castorama: electronic shelf pricing that adjusts prices in real time according to demand
- Tesco: prices products higher in evenings because high-income evening shoppers are willing to pay more
- Coca-Cola (1999 experiment): tested vending machines that automatically raised prices in warmer weather but abandoned the experiment

**Key insight from Robert Crandall (American Airlines):** "If I have 2,000 customers on a given route and 400 different prices, I am obviously short 1,600 prices." Dynamic pricing requires significant technology investments and can backfire if customers feel exploited.

#### 3. **Auction-Based Pricing Model**
The market determines price through competition rather than the seller setting it explicitly. Auctions work best in seller markets with constrained inventory and high demand, allowing sellers to appear neutral while market competition raises prices.

**Examples:**
- Google AdWords: perfected the online ad auction model, accounting for 70% of Google's $35 billion in revenue during the first half of 2015
- eBay: built an $18 billion-a-year business helping people sell household items through auction pricing
- Manheim: world's largest auction provider for buying and selling used cars

#### 4. **Alternative Metric Pricing (Pay-As-You-Go)**
Pricing transactions on measures other than the industry per-unit standard, often closer to product value and customer benefits. This model achieves full monetization potential by aligning the metric directly to how customers perceive value.

**Examples:**
- GE: generates $1 billion annually from outcomes-based services using digital sensors and analytics attached to medical equipment, aircraft engines, and power turbines. Hospitals pay by hours of usage; airlines pay by miles flown.
- Xerox: charges some customers based on usage of printing and copying equipment rather than selling or leasing machines
- Enercon (Germany): charges based on the amount of electricity customers generate using their wind turbines
- Monsanto: uses yield-per-acre pricing for herbicides, maintaining control over product application
- A software company producing lab reports increased revenue by 20% by changing pricing from fixed perpetual license to per lab report
- Robotic surgery equipment manufacturer: could price by surgery instead of upfront cost, aligning with hospital value perception
- Medtech and General Electric: charging hospitals by use of medical equipment rather than selling or renting

**Key advantage:** "You are participating in the revenue your customers generate by using your product and services."

#### 5. **Freemium Model**
A company offers two or more tiers of pricing, one of which is free (also called "land and expand"). The model lowers customer entry cost to zero, spurring rapid adoption and reducing customer acquisition costs.

**Critical limitations:**
- Requires very low production costs and minimal fixed costs to be viable
- Conversion rates are typically below 10% in software companies
- **The land-and-expand approach fails for 90% of companies**
- Video games with freemium models lose 75% of free users after one day and retain only 2% after one month
- "It is extraordinarily difficult to get consumers to buy something they previously received for free"

**Examples:**
- LinkedIn and Dropbox: offer free basic versions with limited features and paid premium tiers
- Evernote: reached 150 million registered users but has been slow to generate revenue
- Most newspapers failed to convert free readers to paid subscribers, except New York Times and Wall Street Journal

### Price Structures

Beyond monetization models, price structures provide flexibility in how charges are applied:

#### **Volume-Based (Tier-Based) Pricing**
Offers better rates as customer usage increases. A one-dimensional structure based on transaction volume.

#### **Matrix Pricing**
A two-dimensional structure where the first dimension is volume and the second dimension is wallet share (percent share of transactions). Higher volume and greater share of business result in better rates for customers.

**Examples:**
- Costco: charges membership (subscription) fee plus product purchase fees
- OpenTable: charges restaurants monthly subscription fee and transaction fee per diner reservation
- Square: 2.5% no-hidden-fee model for startups
- Payment processing service: 3% per transaction with tiered rates based on volume (2% for higher tiers at 100,000–500,000 transactions per month; better rates at 500,000–1.5 million transactions)

### Hybrid and Combined Models

Monetization models can be combined for a mix-and-match approach. For example, Costco combines subscription (membership fee) with transaction-based pricing (product purchases). Adobe moved from selling software by the unit with one-time fees to a cloud-based subscription model.

### Strategic Selection Framework

The chapter emphasizes five key questions for choosing the right monetization model:

1. **What benefits will your customers actually pay for?** Test different models and ensure a functional free experience if using freemium.
2. **How likely are your customers to accept your chosen model?** Customers almost always have preferences for certain pricing models, even when mathematically equivalent options are presented.
3. **How will future developments impact your monetization model?** Conduct scenario planning around trends that could affect your model.
4. **Does your model choice fit your company's current stage and competitive position?** Monetization model choice should suit company lifecycle and competitive position.
5. **How difficult is your chosen monetization model to implement?** Assess data measurement, customer communication, scalability, and total cost of ownership.

## Key Points

- Monetization model (how you charge) is as important as product innovation and pricing strategy
- A bad monetization model can be worse than a bad price
- Five proven monetization models: subscription, dynamic pricing, auction-based, alternative metric, and freemium
- Subscription models lock out competitors and create recurring revenue but require low churn
- Dynamic pricing requires technology investment and can backfire if customers feel exploited
- Auction-based pricing works best in seller markets with constrained inventory
- Alternative metric pricing aligns customer payments with value delivered; GE generates $1 billion annually using this model
- Freemium models fail for 90% of companies attempting them; conversion rates below 10% in software
- Price structures (volume-based and matrix) provide flexibility within monetization models
- Monetization models can be combined for customized approaches
- How you charge trumps how much you charge

## Important Examples and Stories

### Michelin's Ton-Kilometer Transformation
Michelin faced premium price pressure in the early 2000s after inventing longer-lasting tires (20% longer life). A small price increase would cause 20% demand drop. Instead of raising prices, Michelin introduced the ton-kilometer model in 2001, charging trucking fleets by mileage rather than by tires purchased. By 2011, Michelin's earnings were 25% higher than Bridgestone's and 3x Goodyear's—the highest in the industry.

### European Trucking Company Fixed-Price Disaster
A European trucking company signed a three-year contract to distribute TVs at a fixed price per unit. As TV sizes increased exponentially, the distributor could fit fewer TVs per truck and had to make more runs. Costs increased massively while revenue remained flat, resulting in significant losses. This illustrates how a bad monetization model can be worse than a bad price.

### Netflix's Subscription Disruption
Netflix (founded 1999) offered a flat monthly subscription fee for unlimited movie rentals by mail, displacing Blockbuster's per-rental model. By 2012, Netflix had 33 million streaming subscribers; by Q3 2015, 69 million. The subscription model created recurring revenue and customer lock-in.

### GE's Outcomes-Based Services
GE attached digital sensors to medical equipment, aircraft engines, and power turbines, charging by hours of usage for medical practices or miles flown for airlines. This alternative metric model generates $1 billion annually and fully monetizes the value of GE's offerings.

### Google's Ad Auction Model
Google perfected the online ad auction monetization model, which accounted for 70% of Google's $35 billion in revenue during the first half of 2015. The auction model allows Google to appear neutral while market competition raises prices.

### Music Streaming Subscription Growth
Music streaming subscriptions grew six-fold since 2009, generating $1.6 billion in 2014—nearly a quarter of the global music industry's digital revenue. Spotify and Pandora demonstrated the viability of subscription models in the music industry.

## Definitions and Terms

- **Monetization model:** How the customer pays for a new product or service
- **Subscription model:** Customer provides periodic and automatic payment for continued delivery of or access to a company's offering
- **Dynamic pricing:** Pricing model based on factors such as season, time of day, weather, demand, and supply
- **Auction-based pricing:** Market competition determines price rather than seller setting it explicitly
- **Alternative metric pricing:** Pricing transactions on measures other than the industry per-unit standard, often closer to product value and customer benefits
- **Outcomes-based services:** Services where pricing is based on measurable results delivered to customers
- **Freemium model:** Company offers two or more tiers of pricing, one of which is free (also called "land and expand")
- **Land and expand:** Attempting to land with a freemium offer and expand with paid offers
- **Telematics:** Onboard digital devices that track driving behaviors
- **Seller markets:** Markets where inventory is constrained, power is with sellers, and demand is high
- **Two-sided marketplaces:** Those that connect sellers and buyers
- **Volume-based (tier-based) pricing:** Pricing that offers better rates as customer usage increases
- **Matrix pricing:** Two-dimensional pricing structure based on volume and wallet share
- **Wallet share:** Percent share of transactions received from a customer
- **Switching costs:** Costs customers incur when switching to competitors
- **Churn:** Customer attrition or loss
- **Cross-selling:** Selling related products to existing customers
- **Upselling:** Selling higher-value products to existing customers
- **Recurring revenue:** Revenue that repeats at regular intervals
- **Customer lifetime revenue:** Total revenue generated from a customer over their relationship with the company
