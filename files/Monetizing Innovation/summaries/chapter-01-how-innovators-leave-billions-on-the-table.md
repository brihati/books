# CHAPTER 1: How Innovators Leave Billions on the Table

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Core Problem: Monetization Failure

Nearly 75% of new products fail to meet revenue and profit goals, yet this widespread failure stems from a single, preventable mistake: companies neglect customer willingness to pay during product development. Only about 5% of business cases contain information on how much customers will actually pay for a product, leaving revenue estimates as guesses at best. This fundamental disconnect between innovation and monetization leaves billions on the table annually.

### The Contrasting Case Studies: Porsche Cayenne vs. Dodge Dart

The chapter opens with two automotive launches that illustrate the difference between monetization success and failure:

**Porsche's Cayenne (Success):** In the early 1990s, Porsche faced financial crisis with annual sales a third of what they had been in the 1980s. New CEO Wendelin Wiedeking implemented Japanese-style manufacturing to stabilize the company and buy time for fundamental innovation. Rather than designing an SUV based on engineering preferences, Porsche conducted extensive customer surveys before development to understand demand and acceptable price ranges. Every feature was evaluated against customer willingness to pay—large cup holders were included because customers wanted them, while the famous manual six-speed racing transmission was removed because it wasn't on the customer wish list. The result: the Cayenne became Porsche's most profitable vehicle per car in the entire automotive industry, eventually generating 100,000 units annually and half the company's total profit.

**Fiat Chrysler's Dodge Dart (Failure):** In March 2012, Chrysler CEO Sergio Marchionne told workers at the Belvidere, Illinois plant: "Our future hangs on how well we do here" and "Of all the cars I can get wrong, it ain't this one." The company committed hundreds of millions of dollars to develop the Dart from the Fiat Alfa Romeo Giulietta model. However, the car launched with only 25,000 units sold versus 100,000 predicted by market analysts, making it the year's second biggest new product flop according to MarketWatch. By 2014, sales were so disappointing the company issued temporary layoffs at the Belvidere plant. In the first nine months of 2015, Dart sales were only one-seventh of combined sales of two compact segment leaders.

### Why Companies Fail: The Traditional Approach

Most companies follow a flawed sequence: they design the product first, then price it last. This approach treats pricing and marketing as afterthoughts rather than core drivers of product development. The compact car segment—accounting for one in every six vehicles sold in America—demonstrates the stakes: any carmaker unable to succeed in this category was "doomed," yet Fiat Chrysler's massive investment and executive commitment could not overcome the fundamental monetization failure.

### The Four Monetization Failure Modes

A 2014 Simon-Kucher & Partners survey of 2,000 companies found that monetization failures fall into only four predictable categories:

1. **Feature Shock:** Cramming too many features into one product—sometimes even unwanted features—creates a product that doesn't fully resonate with customers and is often overpriced.

2. **Minivation:** An innovation that is the right product for the right market but is priced too low to achieve its full revenue potential.

3. **Hidden Gem:** A potential blockbuster product that is never properly brought to market, generally because it falls outside the core business.

4. **Undead:** An innovation that customers don't want but has nevertheless been brought to market, either because it was the wrong answer to the right question or an answer to a question no one was asking.

### Successful Innovators: A Different Approach

Successful product innovators like LinkedIn, Porsche, Swarovski, Dräger, Optimizely, and Uber share a common pattern: they determine what customers value and what they are willing to pay *before* designing products, then design around these inputs with a clear monetization strategy. Examples include:

- **LinkedIn's Talent Solutions:** Now drives the lion's share of the social networking site's revenue and profits
- **Dräger's Gas Detection Equipment:** For miners and underground workers, achieved sales 250% higher than expected
- **Uber:** Disrupted public transportation with private valuation toward $60 billion by end of 2015

### The Solution: Nine New Rules for Innovation Success

Rather than the traditional "design first, price last" approach, successful innovators follow a flipped process: **Market and price first, then design, then build.** This integrated framework includes nine rules that reinforce each other:

1. Have the willingness-to-pay conversation with customers early in product development
2. Build customer segments based on differences in willingness to pay, not demographics
3. Match product configuration and bundling carefully with meaningful segments
4. Draft business cases using customer willingness-to-pay data
5. Communicate value clearly and compellingly to customers
6. Control discounting tightly and use price cuts only as a last resort
7. Understand that how you charge is often more important than how much you charge
8. Account for customers' full psyches, including emotions, in purchase decisions
9. Link price, value, volume, and cost in business cases to reflect market realities

## Definitions and Terms

- **Price:** Understood as perceived value that the innovation holds for the customer and how much the customer is willing to pay for that value; serves as both an indication of what customers value and a measure of willingness to pay. Price is the single most critical factor in determining whether a product makes money.

- **Willingness to Pay:** The amount customers are actually willing to spend for a product, determined through direct customer research rather than competitor comparison or internal cost-plus calculations.

- **Feature Shock:** Cramming too many features into one product—sometimes even unwanted features—creating a product that doesn't fully resonate with customers and is often overpriced.

- **Minivation:** An innovation that is the right product for the right market but is priced too low to achieve its full revenue potential.

- **Hidden Gem:** A potential blockbuster product that is never properly brought to market, generally because it falls outside the core business.

- **Undead:** An innovation that customers don't want but has nevertheless been brought to market, either because it was the wrong answer to the right question or an answer to a question no one was asking.

- **Monetization:** The process of designing products around customer value and willingness to pay to ensure revenue and profit success.

- **Business Case:** A document outlining product viability; only 5% include customer willingness-to-pay data.

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Conduct extensive customer surveys and price research before beginning product development** — Porsche's approach to understanding acceptable price ranges and demand

2. **Design products around customer-perceived value and willingness to pay, not engineering preferences** — Remove features customers don't value (like Porsche removing the manual transmission); add features they do (like cup holders)

3. **Evaluate every feature against customer demand and willingness to pay for it** — Implement "feature trials" where each element must justify its inclusion

4. **Establish clear monetization strategy early in the innovation process** — Don't treat pricing as an afterthought; integrate it into product development from the start

5. **Flip the traditional product development process: Market and price first, then design, then build** — Reverse the sequence that leads to failure

6. **Determine the market for a new product long before the products are built** — Validate demand before committing resources

7. **Make sure the market is willing to pay for the product before embarking on productizing the innovation** — Test willingness to pay before full development investment

8. **Ask customers what they would pay for a product rather than just comparing to competitors** — Direct research beats indirect benchmarking

9. **Understand in advance how price changes (e.g., 20% increase) would affect demand and volume** — Model price elasticity before launch

10. **Include customer willingness-to-pay information in business cases for new products** — Move from 5% to 100% of business cases containing pricing data

11. **Build customer segments based on differences in willingness to pay, not demographics** — Segment by value perception rather than age, income, or geography

12. **Control discounting tightly and use price cuts only as a last resort** — Avoid eroding margins when demand is weak; instead, revisit product-market fit

13. **Recognize that monetization failures fall into four categories: feature shock, minivation, hidden gem, and undead** — Use this framework to diagnose and prevent failures
