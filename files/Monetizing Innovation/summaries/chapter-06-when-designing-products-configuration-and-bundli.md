# CHAPTER 6: When Designing Products, Configuration and Bundling is More Science Than Art

## Detailed Chapter Summary

Product configuration and bundling are critical, science-based design decisions for monetizing innovation. Configuration involves selecting which features and functionalities to include in a product for each customer segment based on their needs, value perception, and willingness to pay. Bundling means combining products or services together to increase profit and customer satisfaction.

### Core Principles of Product Configuration

Successful configuration requires designing products with only the features customers will pay for, establishing clear distinctions between offerings to prevent cannibalization, and having the discipline to exclude features even when tempted to add value. Companies must curb instincts to please customers by giving away value-added functionality unless customers will pay for it. Product configuration requires the discipline to give low-price segments only basic quality and service levels rather than everything.

The foundation of effective configuration is classifying features into three categories: **leaders** (critical features that drive purchases with high willingness to pay), **fillers** (features of moderate importance or nice-to-haves), and **killers** (features that will blow the deal if customers are forced to pay for them). Killer features should be eliminated from the offering but could be sold à la carte to the few who want them. Features may fall into different categories for different customer segments—for example, heated car seats are a leader feature for drivers in the frigid north but a filler or killer in warm or tropical climates.

### The Good/Better/Best Model

A well-crafted Good/Better/Best (G/B/B) three-tier configuration works because customers avoid extremes and choose the compromise option. The better offer greatly increases the probability that a salesperson closes a deal and maximizes monetizing potential. Ideal customer distribution is 30% in the good tier and 70% in better and best tiers, with the best tier representing at least 10% of customers. If more than 50% of customers buy entry-level products, this indicates the land-and-expand strategy is failing and features should be removed to enable upselling.

The number of product configurations should align with the number of distinct customer segments identified. Once you exceed nine benefits or four products, configurations run the risk of exceeding psychological thresholds and causing feature shock—products with too many features customers don't value. Too many features lead to feature shock; missing valued features create minivations; unwanted features create undead products.

### Bundling Strategies and Revenue Impact

Bundling can significantly increase revenue and profit because customers buy more than they would have without bundling. Mixed bundling (selling products both as stand-alones and bundled) generates more revenue than selling items separately or bundled alone. For example, in a pizza and breadsticks pricing puzzle, separate items generated $3,300 in revenue, bundling only generated $4,200 (27% increase), and mixed bundling generated $4,400 (33% increase). Only about 10% of executives answer this puzzle correctly with the $4,400 figure.

Effective bundling makes purchasing decisions easier for customers and increases satisfaction. It's a win-win: customers get multiple products for less than buying separately, while companies realize more revenue and profit. However, bundling is not always about discounting. If you do your math right across segments, you can often show discounting without actually discounting. Bundle pricing communication can be framed strategically to appeal to customers differently.

Mixed bundling works better than hard bundling in most cases except when you have market power. Hard bundling (where the company does not give customers the option to buy components piecemeal) only works when you are the dominant player. In mixed bundling scenarios, individual prices must be higher than standalone optimal prices. Bundling with integration value can command premium pricing rather than discounts.

### Real-World Examples

**Paper Manufacturer**: Created four segment-based offerings: Core (basic product at affordable price), Product Plus, Logistics Plus (guaranteed next-day delivery of stock products and seven-day delivery of customized products with priority delivery), and Best (combined features of Product Plus and Logistics Plus).

**Regional European Retail Bank**: Had accumulated 30 different account offerings over time. The large number of choices made it harder for the sales force to sell them and customers didn't know which was best. After interviewing customers and identifying three segments (Comfort, Direct, Classic), the bank created three new offerings. Sales skyrocketed after the reduction because each product became distinct and salespeople could articulate each product's value. The bank's personal banking services unit saw a 30% increase in annual profit. The bank had finally made each product distinct; its sales staff could articulate each product's value; and customers could easily determine which one best fit their needs.

**Microsoft Office**: Bundling strategy helped cement customer loyalty and grow market share against competitors like Lotus 1-2-3 and WordPerfect. By 2013, Office was Microsoft's biggest business division, accounting for about one-third of total revenue and more than 40% of operating profit.

**McDonald's Value Meals**: Convince customers who might skip soda or fries to buy the bundle at an attractive price.

**Dropbox**: Offers pro, business, and enterprise products as a G/B/B offering.

### Unbundling as a Strategic Alternative

Unbundling can be a successful strategy when customers are price-sensitive to base offerings. **Ryanair** provides the most compelling case study. Launched in Ireland in 1985, Ryanair struggled initially, then adopted Southwest Airlines' low-cost strategy in the early 1990s by unbundling airline services. The company charged £9.99 base fares but sold baggage (£15 check fee), seat selection (£10 reservation fee), and administration fees (£6, waived for those with a prepaid £6 Ryanair Cash Passport) à la carte, quadrupling the average customer's total cost.

The overwhelming reason Ryanair's unbundling strategy was successful is because customers paid far more attention to the base fare than to surcharges—they are approximately two times more price sensitive to base fares than to surcharges. Unbundling helped Ryanair achieve a much lower base fare, making the offer look very attractive and successfully changing travel habits and customer segments in Europe by making airline travel accessible to lower-income consumers.

By 2014, Ryanair achieved remarkable growth: 50-fold revenue multiplication over three decades (€5 billion annual revenue), 35-fold net profit increase (€522 million in 2014 vs. €15 million in 1995), and €19 billion market capitalization—more than three times Lufthansa's €6.4 billion despite Lufthansa having six times higher revenues.

### Implementation Principles

Clear product differentiation based on segment needs increases sales effectiveness and customer satisfaction. When products are distinct and sales staff can articulate each product's value, customers can easily determine which product best fits their needs, leading to increased sales. Establishing clear "fences" between products prevents cannibalization.

A bundling checklist should address: (1) How well does the configuration advance strategic and tactical goals? (2) Will it enhance company differentiation as a solutions provider? (3) Will it generate awareness for filler products for cross-selling? (4) Does it exceed key psychological price thresholds? (5) Does it avoid including features with very low customer willingness to pay? (6) Do customers get more value using features in combination versus separately? (7) How convenient is it for the customer? (8) Can it be structured to avoid triggering adverse competitive reaction? (9) Does it speak to customers?

## Definitions and Terms

- **Product configuration**: The decision of which features and functionalities will be included in a product (also called packaging in software and tech industries)
- **Bundling**: Combining a product or service with other products and services; selling products together to increase profit and customer satisfaction
- **Mixed bundling**: Selling products both as stand-alones and in a bundle, giving customers choice
- **Hard bundling**: Company does not give customers the option to buy components piecemeal
- **Feature shock**: Products with too many features customers don't value; exceeding psychological thresholds
- **Minivation**: Products missing features customers actually want
- **Undead products**: Products with features customers won't pay for
- **Leaders**: Critical features that drive customers to buy a product; customers have high willingness to pay for leader features
- **Fillers**: Features of moderate importance or nice-to-haves
- **Killers**: Features that will blow the deal if the customer is forced to pay for them
- **Good/Better/Best (G/B/B)**: A three-tier product configuration model where good has core features, better is intermediate, and best has all features
- **Land and expand**: A strategy focused first on acquiring customers with entry-level products and then upselling them to better versions
- **Willingness to pay (WTP)**: Customer's maximum price they will pay for a feature or product
- **Cannibalization**: When a higher-priced product offering takes sales from a lower-priced offering due to unclear differentiation
- **Fences between products**: Clear differentiation that prevents cannibalization between product tiers
- **À la carte pricing**: Selling individual components separately rather than bundled
- **Integration value**: Added value from using features or products in combination versus separately
- **Unbundling**: Separating bundled products and selling components individually
