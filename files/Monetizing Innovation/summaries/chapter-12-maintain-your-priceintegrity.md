# CHAPTER 12: Maintain Your Price Integrity

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Core Problem: Knee-Jerk Price Reductions

Price integrity—maintaining carefully set pricing despite market pressures—is critical to preserving profit margins and brand value. When new products underperform in early market reception, companies often respond with knee-jerk price reductions. This is a fundamental mistake. Cutting prices in response to weak early sales sends an unintended message that the product has less value than initially communicated, and price reductions cannot be easily repaired, resulting in permanent profit loss over customer lifetime value.

The first few months after launch are critical for establishing the pricing narrative about product value. Reducing price soon after launch tells potential buyers the company made a mistake in quality or otherwise. Even if there is a quality problem, a price cut won't fix it and could make matters worse. Knee-jerk price reductions are common because teams feel pressure to do something immediately when sales disappoint, but successful innovators must remain calm and analyze the situation rather than overreact with promotions or price cuts.

### The Apple Watch Case Study

Apple's April 2015 launch of the Apple Watch provides a compelling counterexample. Priced at $349 for the cheapest version, up to $17,000 for luxury versions, the product received largely negative reactions. A Wall Street Journal headline stated "Glimmers Emerge on Apple Watch Sales, and They're Not Pretty." Stock analysts noted that components suppliers produced fewer units than projected. Yahoo's David Pogue wrote "You don't need one," while Joanna Stern gave a positive review but advised consumers to wait for future releases. Despite this pressure, Apple held firm on pricing. By October 2015, the entry price remained $349. The company estimated 8 million watches sold in the first six months, generating approximately $2.8 billion in revenue.

### Post-Launch Problem Diagnosis

A firm's pricing strategy can collapse in a single day if unprepared for sales lag. Post-launch problems typically come in four varieties: the market doesn't understand the product's value, competition undercuts price, competition launches a competing product, or sales fall below plan. Being patient doesn't mean doing nothing; it requires all-hands-on-deck action to determine cause and solution.

A European company's pricing strategy fell apart in one day when a sales manager was given freedom to make any pricing cuts he felt necessary until year-end. The CEO authorized pricing cuts across Europe for the rest of the year, causing the firm's pricing strategy to collapse in a single day. This illustrates why preparation and process are essential.

### Identifying Root Causes Through Analysis

Before approving price cuts, companies must conduct thorough cross-functional analysis. A pricing problem may actually be a communication, quality, or sales-force training problem. An industrial products manufacturer discovered a competitor's preemptive price cut with huge temporary discounts and high minimum purchase orders caused disappointing sales of their new product. Customers stocked up on the competitor's discounted product and had no room for the new one despite its better quality.

BMW in 2001 traced slow sales of its new 7 Series sedans to European customers thinking it was ugly. The company kept the price high, reduced sales targets instead, and worked on a face-lift. This demonstrates the importance of diagnosing the actual problem before resorting to price cuts.

### Tracking Comprehensive Metrics

Most companies track only financial KPIs but must also track sales, customer, and operational metrics. Key metrics include:
- **Sales KPIs**: win-loss ratio, percent deviation of final price from target price, average sales quoting time, and price as a reason for win/loss
- **Escalated deals KPIs**: number of escalated deals, price changes upon escalation, and rejected escalated requests
- **Customer segmentation metrics**: price difference achieved across segments and feature usage

A healthy escalation process should have pushbacks on 30-40% of deals. At most 20-30% of deals should be escalated, depending on industry.

### Deal Deconstructions as Learning Tools

Deal deconstructions involve dissecting the reasons why you're winning and losing deals by bringing together a cross-functional team to fully deconstruct the deal and understand whether product strategy, price strategy, and value communications were applied correctly. These should be framed as learning experiences with no repercussions to encourage open and honest assessment.

### The Non-Pricing Alternatives Requirement

Before approving a pricing reduction, require your team to come up with three non-pricing alternatives. A telecommunications firm in Latin America instituted a rule requiring managers to come up with three non-price actions before making price cuts. This simple rule had the highest impact and instilled the right discipline. If a price cut must be approved, the team must explain why it is superior to the non-pricing alternatives.

If you must lower price, ask customers for something in return: longer-term commitment, greater volume, introductions to new departments, endorsements, or joint press releases.

### The Danger of High Sales: Underpricing

Unusually high sales could indicate pricing lower than necessary. Companies should treat unusually high sales as a potential problem indicating underpricing and examine it as rigorously as disappointing sales.

The Mercedes SL automobile case illustrates this principle. In the 1970s, the model sold out in a few months with a two-year waiting list, indicating the price was about 20 percent too low. Mercedes could only raise the price 3 to 5 percent per year, leaving hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue on the table over several years. PlayMobil similarly misjudged its market size when demand outstripped supply.

When raising prices after discovering underpricing, do it carefully in several steps and accompany increases with small product improvements to prevent customer backlash.

### Price Wars: A Destructive Dynamic to Avoid

Price wars have only one winner: the supplier with the lowest cost. An overwhelming 90 percent of companies fighting price wars claim competitors started it, indicating denial about price war causation. War-game your major competitors' reactions to your pricing moves by writing down expected reactions and simulating your position afterward.

Innovation leaders avoid price wars at nearly all costs because it's a slippery slope. You don't want to start one, and you don't want to be the first one to move. Ensure clear communications to avoid misunderstandings that provoke price wars.

### Pricing Patience as Organizational Capability

Showing patience about pricing is more important than fretting over sales numbers in the first months after a product launch. Pricing patience is an important capability of every organization. But as simple as it sounds, it is the most difficult thing to get across to executive leadership. If you haven't anticipated the possibility that sales might lag and you haven't planned a response, this is no time to overreact. This is a time to stop and think.

## Definitions and Terms

- **Price Integrity**: Maintaining the carefully set price despite market pressures, avoiding knee-jerk price reductions in response to lukewarm early market reactions
- **Losing Price Integrity**: When a company's knee-jerk response to a lukewarm early market reaction is to reduce the product's price in the first few months
- **Deal Deconstructions**: Dissecting the reasons why you're winning and losing deals by bringing together a cross-functional team to fully deconstruct the deal and understand whether product strategy, price strategy, and value communications were applied correctly
- **Pricing Patience**: An important organizational capability to resist the temptation to lower prices quickly and to remain calm and analytical when facing disappointing early sales
- **Non-Pricing Alternatives**: Actions other than price reduction to address sales problems, such as increasing advertising, adding product value, improving communications, or enhancing sales training
- **Price War**: Destructive competitive dynamic where competitors engage in successive price cuts; only the lowest-cost supplier can win
- **Price Elasticity**: The relationship between price changes and demand; should be included in forecasting models
- **Customer Lifetime Value**: The total profit a company expects to earn from a customer over the entire relationship; eroded by price cuts
- **Win-Loss Ratio**: Sales KPI tracking the proportion of deals won versus lost
- **Price Deviation**: Percent deviation of final price from target price; a key sales KPI
- **Deal Escalation**: Process where pricing decisions are escalated for approval; should have 20-30% escalation rate with 30-40% pushback rate

## Actionable Lessons

### Before Approving Price Cuts
1. Don't cut prices in the first few weeks after launch when sales disappoint; wait to analyze what happened
2. Before approving a pricing reduction, require your team to come up with three non-pricing alternatives
3. If a price cut must be approved, the team must explain why it is superior to the non-pricing alternatives
4. Reference your price elasticity curve and business case before making any price reductions to understand the profit impact
5. If forced to cut prices, plan it strategically and ensure you get something in return to preserve price integrity

### Conducting Root Cause Analysis
6. Don't assume the wrong price was set on the product without thorough analysis
7. Recognize that a pricing problem may actually be a communication, quality, or sales-force training problem
8. Conduct deal deconstructions with cross-functional teams to identify weaknesses and best practices
9. Frame deal deconstructions as learning experiences with no repercussions to encourage open and honest assessment
10. War-game your major competitors' reactions to your pricing moves by writing down expected reactions and simulating your position afterward

### Tracking and Accountability
11. Track sales KPIs such as win-loss ratio, percent deviation of final price from target price, average sales quoting time, and price as a reason for win/loss

13. Track price difference achieved across segments and feature usage to assess customer segmentation strategy effectiveness
14. Go beyond financial KPIs and track monthly outcomes including sales, customer, and operational metrics
15. Put in place processes for regularly updating models and forecasts with new market information and make people accountable for delivering on forecasts
16. Ensure models and forecasts include price elasticity assumptions

17. Treat unusually high sales as a potential problem indicating underpricing; examine it as rigorously as disappointing sales
