# CHAPTER 10: The Innovation Won't Speak for Itself

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### The Core Problem: Disconnected Communication Teams

Successful product launches require strong marketing and sales communication of value to customers, yet most companies struggle because communication teams are detached from the innovation process and arrive too late. The fundamental principle is simple: **customers buy benefits, not products**. However, translating this principle into practice requires deliberate effort and early integration of sales and marketing teams into product development.

The challenge is acute given the time constraints of modern customer engagement. Salespeople typically have only **10 minutes** with customers, advertisements run for **15 seconds**, and websites receive **5 minutes** of customer attention. Within these narrow windows, companies must articulate clear benefits rather than features and speak the customer's language rather than internal company jargon.

### Why Value Messaging Fails

Three structural problems prevent effective value communication:

1. **Late involvement of frontline teams**: Sales and marketing teams lack understanding of the value-to-customer story that drove product design when they join after development concludes.

2. **Internal politics override customer focus**: The loudest or most senior voices in meetings often win the message-framing battle, resulting in messages that don't match the original value proposition.

3. **Feature confusion**: Too many features confuse customers and prevent purchase decisions. Companies struggle to distinguish between features (which belong to the product) and benefits (which belong to the customer).

### The SmugMug Case Study: Transformation Through Simplification

SmugMug, a photography platform, exemplifies both the problem and the solution. Despite receiving praise from prestigious publications—the Wall Street Journal called it a "strikingly handsome photography site" and Forbes recognized it as "one of the top company brands in its field and one of the most-liked and most-respected personal brands"—sales were not picking up due to poor value communication and customer confusion.

Cofounders Chris and Don MacAskill restructured their value messaging by condensing benefit statements from **over 100 features to fewer than 10 benefits**, including "beautiful design" and "unlimited storage." They reorganized their offering into four clear plans (basic, power, portfolio, business), each addressing specific customer needs, and moved detailed feature comparisons to an optional section. This transformation resulted in a **double-digit percentage increase in revenue and conversion**.

### Quantifying Value: The SaaS Supply Chain Example

A SaaS supply chain company demonstrated another effective approach by creating a spreadsheet tool for salespeople to quantify customer payback. The tool calculated savings from multiple sources: automated picking, reduced inventory carrying costs, eliminated shipping errors, and paper documentation elimination. By translating features into dollar-and-cents value, the company made pricing justifiable to customers and enabled salespeople to focus conversations on value rather than price.

### The Matrix of Competitive Advantages (MOCA)

To structure value communications effectively, companies should use the Matrix of Competitive Advantages (MOCA), a 2×2 matrix with relative importance of benefits on the y-axis and performance against competition on the x-axis. This framework helps identify which benefits to emphasize:

- **Top right quadrant** (important to customers AND where you outperform competitors): These are primary selling points.
- **Top left quadrant** (important to customers BUT where you underperform): These require defensive arguments.
- Other quadrants: Lower priority for messaging.

### Segment-Specific Messaging

The same value messages do not work for all customer segments. Examples include:

- **Adobe Creative Cloud**: Uses different messages for Individuals ("getting all the creative apps," "search 45 million stock images and videos") versus Enterprise ("customized provisioning deployment," "enterprise level support," "streamlined license management and deployment").
- **LinkedIn Talent Solutions**: Focuses on "Find and engage the best passive talent."
- **Mini car company**: Creates specific value messages for each of its eight models.
- **Carbonite**: Changed its description from "20 gigabytes" (technical specification) to "unlimited storage" (customer benefit).

### The Innovation Process Is Not the Message

Customers are not interested in how innovations were created or how much was spent on them. Thomas Edison's incandescent bulb sold not because of his innovation process or the thousand filaments tested, but because it solved the problem of tripping over toys in the dark. Similarly, a company claiming "The product is good because it has seven patents" misses the customer's perspective entirely.

### Integration and Measurement

Effective value communication requires:

1. **Early integration**: Add key marketing and sales people to innovation teams from the very start to ensure they understand the value messages that resonated with customers.

2. **Customer-centric questioning**: For each feature, ask: "What does the customer achieve because of this feature?" Probe customers about their pain points and how your product solves them. Understand how customers measure their performance and how your offering would affect those measures.

3. **Continuous refinement**: Run MOCA analysis regularly to measure customer perceptions and refine messaging. Test value messages with customers before finalizing them.

4. **Restraint**: Practice restraint and avoid jamming every feature into marketing communications. Keep value messages extremely brief and simple, using language customers understand rather than technical jargon.

## Key Points

- Customers buy benefits, not products or features
- Sales interactions are brief (10 minutes), advertisements are short (15 seconds), website visits are limited (5 minutes)
- Marketing and sales teams must be involved early in product development to avoid detachment from the innovation process
- A feature belongs to the product; a benefit belongs to the customer
- Great new products don't sell themselves; marketing must promote them and sales must sell them
- Quantifying value in dollars and cents makes pricing justifiable to customers
- The same value messages do not work for all customer segments
- Technical specifications mean nothing to average consumers without context
- The Matrix of Competitive Advantages (MOCA) helps structure value communications
- Customers care about solutions to their problems, not the innovation process itself

## Definitions and Terms

- **Feature**: Belongs to the product; a characteristic or capability
- **Benefit**: Belongs to the customer; what the customer achieves because of a feature
- **Value**: A measure of the benefit to the customer
- **Value communications**: Marketing and sales messaging that articulates customer benefits
- **Matrix of Competitive Advantages (MOCA)**: A 2×2 matrix with relative importance of benefits on y-axis and performance against competition on x-axis
- **SaaS (Software-as-a-Service)**: Software that streamlines and optimizes operations for customers
- **Per-user pricing**: Pricing model based on number of users (noted as suboptimal when product delivers clear quantifiable value)
- **Payback**: Return on investment; quantified savings or benefits to customer
- **Passive job candidates**: Employed individuals not actively seeking new positions (LinkedIn context)

## Actionable Lessons

1. **Integrate early**: Add key marketing and sales people to innovation teams from the very start to ensure they understand the value messages that resonated with customers.

2. **Translate features to benefits**: For each feature, ask: "What does the customer achieve because of this feature?" Translate technical specifications into meaningful customer benefits.

3. **Understand customer pain points**: Probe customers about their pain points and how your product solves them. Understand how customers measure their performance and how your offering would affect those measures.

4. **Use MOCA analysis**: Apply the Matrix of Competitive Advantages to identify which benefits to emphasize in sales and marketing messages. Focus on benefits in the top right quadrant (important to customers and where you outperform competitors). Prepare defensive arguments for competitive disadvantages (top left quadrant).

5. **Quantify value**: Create quantifiable value tools (like spreadsheets) that show customers their return on investment in dollars and cents. This makes pricing justifiable and enables salespeople to focus on value rather than price.

6. **Simplify messaging**: Condense features into fewer, clearer benefit statements. Move detailed feature comparisons to optional sections for interested customers. Practice restraint and avoid jamming every feature into marketing communications.

7. **Segment your messaging**: Create segment-specific value messages tailored to the needs of each customer group. The same messages do not work for all segments.

8. **Use customer language**: Speak the customer's language, not internal company language or technical jargon. Avoid technical specifications that average customers cannot understand.

9. **Keep messages brief and simple**: Given time constraints (10 minutes for sales, 15 seconds for ads, 5 minutes for websites), communicate only the core benefits that matter most to customers.

10. **Test and measure**: Test value messages with customers before finalizing them. Run MOCA analysis regularly to measure customer perceptions and refine messaging. Establish processes to measure the effectiveness of value messages.

11. **Focus on solutions, not process**: Communicate the problems your product solves and the benefits customers receive, not how the innovation was created or how much was spent on it.

12. **Enable sales conversations**: Structure value communications to enable salespeople to focus conversations on value rather than price.
