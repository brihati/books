# CHAPTER 2: Feature Shocks, Minivations, Hidden Gems, and Undeads

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter introduces a framework for understanding four critical categories of monetizing innovation failures: **feature shocks**, **minivations**, **hidden gems**, and **undeads**. Each represents a distinct way that companies fail to capture value from innovation, and each correlates with specific organizational cultures and decision-making patterns.

### Feature Shocks

Feature shocks occur when companies cram too many features into a single product, creating confusing, expensive offerings that please few customers. These products typically emerge from companies with strong product-driven or engineering cultures where ambitious goals lack clear customer focus. The defining characteristic is that R&D teams keep saying "let's add this!" without being able to articulate the product's value to customers. Warning signs include "inside-out" comments—statements reflecting what people inside the company believe is valuable rather than what customers actually need.

The **Amazon Fire Phone** (July 2014, priced at $199 with contract or $649 without) exemplifies feature shock failure. The device featured Dynamic Perspective technology with four camera lenses, facial recognition, the Mayday service, and Firefly shopping capability. Despite technical sophistication, the product lacked standout features and failed to resonate with customers. Within four months, Amazon slashed the price to 99 cents with contract or $449 without, resulting in a $170 million write-down largely from unsold inventory. As one reviewer noted: "The Fire's defining features are fun, but I can't help but feel as though they're merely gimmicks designed by Amazon to demonstrate the company's brilliance—and at the expense of battery life, to boot."

Feature shock products share common characteristics: they are costly to make, overengineered, hard to explain, and usually overpriced. The product's value becomes less than the sum of its parts. Customers typically use only 20 percent of features in complex products, and when faced with feature shock products, they often respond tepidly, leading to price cuts and potential downward death spirals.

### Minivations

Minivations are products that tap neither a product concept's full market potential nor its full price potential. These are "tame, safe answers" that enter the market underpriced or with volume targets that are too low. Unlike feature shocks, minivations don't end in epic failures—they simply fail to capture available value, making them difficult to guard against.

The root cause lies in how companies approach pricing. Rather than asking "What value does this product bring to customers and what portion of that value can we capture?", companies ask "What does this cost to make, and what minimum margin do I need to add?" This cost-plus approach leaves significant money on the table.

**Real-world examples illustrate the magnitude of lost opportunity:**

- A **Silicon Valley component maker** created a breakthrough next-generation product with no peer, priced at 85 cents using cost-plus methodology. A key customer incorporated it into a premium product line and later analysis revealed the component enabled the customer to charge a $50 premium, largely attributable to the new component's functionality. The component could have been priced up to $5, representing a massive undermonetization.

- **Playmobil's 2003 Noah's Ark** play set sold out in two months and resold on eBay for 33 percent higher than the original price, signaling underpricing.

- **Asus eee PC mini-notebook** (€299 in 2008) almost sold out days after launch in Germany, with demand exceeding supply by 900 percent.

- **Audi Q7 luxury SUV** (€55,000 in Q1 2006) had annual demand of 80,000 units but production capacity of only 70,000. Analysis showed the optimal price would have been €58,000, generating an extra €210,000,000 annually.

- **Valeo's Park Assist system** sold to Volkswagen for €100 per unit but VW priced it at €670 to end customers, demonstrating the value gap.

Warning signs of minivations include sales teams easily meeting targets, channel partners reaping maximum margins, and sellouts occurring. The challenge is that "failure can masquerade as success" when products sell well at underpriced levels.

### Hidden Gems

Hidden gems are brilliant or revolutionary ideas that companies fail to recognize or quantify in terms of value to customers. These products often end up in limbo—neither launched nor killed—or arrive undervalued as freebies. Revolutionary product ideas may seem tangential to a company's core business, causing them to be overlooked by midlevel executives who may stop them from reaching the executive suite.

The **Kodak digital camera** represents the most significant hidden gem failure. Steven Sasson, a young Kodak engineer, invented digital camera technology in 1974 in the company's applied research lab. Kodak patented the technology, but because the digital camera threatened Kodak's core film business, it remained hidden. Kodak's bosses never got excited about it. The company didn't introduce its first digital camera (DC40) until 1995—21 years later—and didn't get serious about digital cameras until 2001. Kodak declared bankruptcy in 2012, emerged in 2013, and by 2015 was trying to resurrect its business by licensing patents and technologies.

Another example: a **warehouse conveyor belt manufacturer** developed software that dramatically improved goods flow but gave it away because selling software was foreign to a hardware manufacturer.

Hidden gems are more likely to emerge during business model changes, industry disruptions, or shifts from offline to online, product to service, analog to digital, or hardware to software. Organizations struggle to separate the technically feasible from the commercially practical, and most have no one accountable for recognizing hidden gems.

### Undeads

Undead products are technically sophisticated innovations that still exist in the marketplace but have virtually no demand. They are the wrong answer to the right question—or answers to questions not enough people care about. Undeads result from wildly overstating customer appeal, failing to segment the customer base effectively, and delaying willingness-to-pay conversations until overinvestment makes cancellation difficult.

**The Segway PT** exemplifies the undead category. Dean Kamen unveiled his breakthrough personal transporter in December 2001 after tremendous prelaunch hype. He projected 50,000 sales in the first year, but by the end of 2007—six years later—the company had sold only about 30,000 total. Priced at $3,000–$7,000, the Segway was lampooned in the hit U.S. TV cartoon The Simpsons over several episodes. The company quietly faded from public eye and was sold to Chinese firm Ninebot in April 2015. The Segway found a niche with tour operators and security organizations, but never achieved mainstream adoption.

**Google Glass** (unveiled 2012, priced at $1,500) was intended to create enthusiasm among digerati and journalists who would urge others to buy it, but reviewers panned it instead. The device was criticized for short battery life and privacy concerns, and was banned in theaters, bars, and casinos. Google discontinued the product in January 2015.

Other notable undeads include:
- **New Coke** (1985): Coca-Cola tweaked its recipe for the first time in almost a century; consumers rejected the new taste and disappearance of original; Coke Classic returned within three months.
- **Inhaled insulin**: Required five to ten times more insulin than injected form and was priced three to four times higher; patients continued injecting insulin instead.
- **McDonald's Arch Deluxe burger**, **Microsoft's Zune music player**, **Harley-Davidson perfume**, **Procter & Gamble's Touch of Yogurt Shampoo**, and **Rocky Mountain Sparkling Water from Coors** also exemplify undead products.

The organizational culture plays a major role in creating undeads. Companies fail to ask customers about willingness to pay before engineering plans are drafted. Had firms identified market size by segment and determined who would pay most and least, they would have reformulated or scrapped products before financial damage occurred. In environments where raising concerns risks careers, teams execute flawed projects without speaking truth about undeads.

### Avoidability

The chapter concludes that all four monetization failures are entirely avoidable when organizations understand and apply nine rules of monetizing innovation. Customer-perceived value must be identified long before product launch, engineering, and production. Pricing considerations based on real customer input must be brought into new product development early. The critical insight is that "bringing pricing considerations—based on real customer input—into the new product development process early is critical to avoiding disastrous feature shocks, minivations, hidden gems, and undeads."

## Key Points

1. **Four distinct monetization failure categories** exist: feature shocks (too many features), minivations (underpriced), hidden gems (unrecognized value), and undeads (no demand).

2. **Feature shocks** result from ambitious goals without clear customer focus; R&D teams add features without articulating customer value.

3. **Minivations** emerge from cost-plus pricing rather than value-based pricing; they leave significant money on the table.

4. **Hidden gems** are revolutionary ideas overlooked because they seem tangential to core business or threaten existing revenue streams.

5. **Undeads** are technically sophisticated but answer questions no one is asking; they result from delayed willingness-to-pay conversations.

6. **Organizational culture** significantly influences which failures occur; "good enough" thinking and delayed pricing conversations enable minivations and undeads.

7. **Early customer input** on value and willingness to pay is critical to avoiding all four failure types.

8. **Inside-out thinking** (company perspective rather than customer perspective) is a warning sign of feature shocks.

9. **Price is a critical factor** in undead products—they are far more expensive than alternatives.

10. **Employees often don't speak truth** about flawed products due to career risk concerns.

## Definitions and Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Feature Shock** | When you try to cram too many features into one product, creating a confusing and often expensive mess that pleases few customers; products that cannot articulate a clear value proposition, tend to be one-size-fits-all, have too many "nice to haves" and too few "gotta haves" |
| **Minivation** | Products that tap neither a product concept's full market potential nor its full price potential; tame, safe answers that enter the market underpriced or with volume targets that are too low |
| **Hidden Gem** | A brilliant, even revolutionary idea that a company fails to both recognize and quantify in terms of product value to customers, or decides it lacks capabilities to bring to market |
| **Undead Product** | A product that still exists in the marketplace but demand is virtually nonexistent; the product is dead yet continues to "walk around" like a zombie; the wrong answer to the right question or an answer to a question not enough people care about |
| **Inside-Out Comments** | Comments reflecting what people inside a company believe is valuable about their firm's product rather than customer perspective |
| **Cost-Plus Pricing** | Pricing method that asks "What does this cost to make, and what minimum margin do I need to add on top of that?" rather than value-based pricing |
| **Value Proposition** | Clear articulation of why a product helps customers and what value it delivers |
| **Willingness to Pay** | The maximum price customers will pay for a product based on the value they perceive it delivers |
| **Market Segmentation** | Dividing customers into groups based on different needs and willingness to pay |
| **Digerati** | Early adopters and technology enthusiasts; digital intelligentsia |
| **Downward Death Spiral** | Situation where price cuts lead to reduced margins, which leads to further price cuts in a self-reinforcing negative cycle |
