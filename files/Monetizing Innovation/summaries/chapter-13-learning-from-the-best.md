# CHAPTER 13: Learning from the Best

## Detailed Chapter Summary

Chapter 13 presents seven case studies demonstrating how companies successfully monetized innovation by designing products around a price point rather than pricing products after development. The chapter spans B2B and B2C sectors, traditional and disruptive innovations across automotive, software, and pharmaceutical industries.

### Porsche: Premium-First Launch and Customer-Centric Design

Porsche exemplifies the "design the product around the price" principle through its expansion beyond sports cars. The company launched the Cayenne SUV (2002) and Panamera sedan (2009) using rigorous customer research conducted four years before product introduction. Porsche conducted high-level telephone surveys in important markets and detailed "car clinics"—exhibition halls where potential customers evaluated competitors' vehicles alongside Porsche prototypes.

Porsche's approach differs fundamentally from industry practice: features must justify inclusion rather than justify exclusion. The company measures willingness to pay (WTP) for specific features and classifies them as standard or optional based on customer data. A notable example: initial designs for small hidden cup holders in the Panamera dashboard proved insufficient; customer research revealed demand for much more, leading to an expensive middle console redesign.

The company employed a skimming strategy, launching premium eight-cylinder models first before introducing less expensive six-cylinder variants one year later. This captured high-margin early adopters. By 2014, Porsche sold more than twice as many non-sports cars as sports cars (approximately 135,000 Cayennes, Panameras, and Macans), demonstrating successful transformation. The Cayenne achieved 73,000 sales in 2015; the Panamera sold 22,000 units in 2014—1,000 more than Boxster sales that year.

Porsche's C-suite actively participated in customer research, test-driving competitors' cars and attending focus groups to signal organizational commitment. All relevant functions (sales, marketing, product, strategy, after-sales, market intelligence, finance, controlling) were represented in new product teams for holistic evaluation. Business cases became living documents continually updated after launch.

### LinkedIn: Members-First Monetization in Two-Sided Marketplaces

LinkedIn built its monetization strategy around a "members first" principle, exemplified by the InMail feature priced at $10 per message with a response guarantee to preserve marketplace quality and prevent spam. In January 2015—12 years after InMail's launch—LinkedIn reversed its policy to credit back messages without responses within 90 days, discouraging low-quality generic messages. This timing was viable only when the platform had hundreds of millions of members.

LinkedIn monetizes both sides of its two-sided marketplace—a rare approach. The Talent Solutions unit grew to $1.3 billion by 2014, with 41% revenue increase in 2015. Premium member subscriptions contributed 18% of total revenue in 2015. LinkedIn's rigorous monetization process embeds a multi-step, iterative approach: hypothesis development, internal refinement, initial customer validation, gut-check, building a precise model, and paid pilots.

The company uses paid pilots instead of free beta testing to validate monetization potential and gather better feedback. LinkedIn's recruiting-tools side achieved 36-fold revenue growth in five years, demonstrating the power of customer-centric monetization.

### Dräger Safety: Voice-of-Customer Research and Structured Innovation

Dräger Safety transformed from an unstructured, internally-driven innovation process relying on anecdotal sales feedback to a customer-centric methodology. Under CEO Ralf Drews (promoted in 2008 after joining as mechanical engineer in 1991), the company shifted to direct field observation of customers and their unarticulated needs.

The old process created overengineered, feature-laden products with scope creep and mediocre margins. Sales feedback was anecdotal, reflected only articulated needs, lacked prioritization, and wasn't direct from customers. The new process began with direct field observation. Drews and his team observed oil and gas drilling workers and sewage workers in Hamburg's canals who spent 80-90% of job time underground.

This research revealed two main problems addressed by the X-zone 5000 gas detector: pedestrians kicking detectors into canals and insufficient alarm visibility/volume in rain. The X-zone 5000 achieved 250% higher sales than expected with a 35% price premium. The sewage market version came in a more expensive watertight case because sewage customers valued this feature and had high WTP, while petrochemicals customers showed less interest and almost no WTP.

Dräger Safety institutionalized the Customer Process Monitoring (CPM) approach, a five-phase research process beginning before product design: defining target markets, identifying key decision makers, conducting qualitative research, performing quantitative surveys with WTP analysis, and assessing competitive products. The company creates tailored selling stories for different customer roles and tests market reactions before product development begins.

CPM eliminated the need for major product specification changes during development. All new Dräger Safety innovations must now go through CPM for approval. The Alcotest product (alcohol breath-detecting device) achieved 10 times higher sales than the version it replaced with significantly greater profit margins.

### Uber: Dynamic Pricing and Penetration Strategy

Uber achieved one of the highest valuations of any U.S. company in 5.5 years without owning cars or inventory. Its success was powered by revolutionary monetization through dynamic pricing and penetration pricing. Price elasticity was "off the charts" for both riders and drivers, making pricing the key lever for solving supply and demand problems.

Dynamic pricing increases supply and reduces demand to maximize availability and reliability. Surge pricing was poorly named; "availability pricing" would better communicate value to customers. Uber offers additional money to drivers to pick up weekend bar patrons after last call in Boston at 2 a.m., demonstrating how pricing incentivizes supply.

Penetration pricing with low margins requires rapid scaling to succeed. Lower prices expand the addressable market by 20 times compared to premium pricing. Uber positions itself as alternative to public transportation, rental cars, car ownership, and even walking. UberPool increases driver utilization by combining multiple riders, making service more affordable.

Customer segmentation allows Uber to serve different willingness-to-pay levels: UberBlack for premium convenience seekers, UberX for no-frills riders, UberASSIST for elderly and differently abled, and UberChopper for time-critical travel. The no-tipping policy is intentional design to avoid customer friction—removing the tipping mechanism reduces consumer anxiety and decision-making burden.

### Swarovski: Value-Driven Pricing and Brand Monetization

Swarovski conducted research across 20+ countries to identify drivers of customer willingness to pay. Cut complexity and brilliance significantly increase customer WTP for crystals. Customers willing to pay 5-6 times more for sophisticated cuts versus traditional ones.

The company implemented five price tiers (Essential, Classic, Advanced, Sophisticated, Outstanding) creating a 650% price span from lowest to highest tier. Before this process, Swarovski based prices on "internal gut feeling" rather than customer value, resulting in wrong prices and steep discounts.

The Custom Solutions unit conducts detailed end-consumer research to understand WTP for customized crystal products. Consumers pay 120% premium for Swarovski-embellished smartphone cases; premiums vary from 30% to several hundred percent for other products. Swarovski monetizes its brand and logo through spillover effects with weaker-brand manufacturers—furniture makers use Swarovski crystals on lamps and chairs to stand out in stores.

Implementation required 1-2 additional years to complete the monetization journey after initial 2-year adoption period. A pricing board with clear mandate effectively guided monetization transformation. The company changed from price-based to value-based product design through structured governance.

### Optimizely: Usage-Based Pricing and Product Sequencing

Optimizely's monetization strategy demonstrates that how to charge can be more important than how much to charge. The company chose usage-based pricing (Monthly Unique Visitors—MUVs) over per-user pricing because per-user pricing had no relationship to software value. MUVs metric aligned better with product value and enabled smaller companies to afford Optimizely and grow into higher tiers.

Dan Siroker's experience as director of analytics for the 2008 Obama presidential campaign led to Optimizely's founding. A/B testing of web pages, emails, and communications boosted donations and signups. The "learn more" button outperformed "sign up" or "join us now"; a black and white picture of Obama with family boosted engagement; combined button and image test increased email signups by 40%.

Customer validation revealed different needs for Testing and Personalization products. Prospects preferred to sequence purchases rather than buy bundled. Keeping products separate but integrated allowed customers to mix and match packages. Testing product uses MUV metric; Personalization product uses total site traffic metric because Personalization requires constant, universal application unlike Testing which is periodic.

### Pharmaceutical Company: Customer Value-Driven R&D

A pharmaceutical company restructured its drug development process to integrate local market teams early, determine clinical and economic value by Phase II, and increase cooperation between clinical development, marketing, and market access. This customer value-driven approach enabled the company to kill more products early, concentrate resources on fewer high-potential compounds, and reduce commercial risk.

Pharmaceutical companies spend an average of $2.6 billion to get a single new prescription drug to market. Traditional pharma R&D focused on clinical success; modern approach requires commercial viability assessment. Local country teams serve as surrogates for customer input on market access and pricing when primary research is prohibitively expensive.

The company changed R&D incentive structures from speed and positive outcomes to financial minimums. Weaker candidates are now dropped earlier to allocate resources to better candidates. Success redefined from regulatory approval alone to market viability and payer value. Products stopped due to lack of differentiation against standard treatment or lack of added value in eyes of key payers.

## Definitions and Terms

- **Design the product around the price:** Approach to monetizing innovation where price point is determined first, then product features are designed to meet that price
- **Willingness to Pay (WTP):** Maximum price consumers are willing to pay for a product based on perceived value
- **Car clinics:** Exhibition halls where Porsche rented competitors' cars and presented them alongside new Porsche models for potential customer evaluation
- **Value analysis:** Process to determine which features customers want and are willing to pay for
- **CPM (Customer Process Monitoring):** New product development process beginning before product design with five research phases focused on understanding customer needs and WTP
- **Buying center:** Group consisting of safety engineer, procurement professional, and technical/plant manager, each with different requirements and influence on buying decision
- **Needs-based segments:** Customer groups with homogeneous requirements, values, and WTP
- **Dynamic pricing:** Charging customers more during peak demand periods to increase supply and manage demand
- **Surge pricing:** Dynamic pricing mechanism charging customers more during peak demand
- **Penetration pricing:** Pricing low on purpose to gain large market share, operating at razor-thin margins
- **Utilization rate:** Percentage of time a driver actually has a customer in the car
- **Two-sided marketplace:** Platform serving both members (who create profiles and build professional connections) and recruiters
- **Monetization model:** Structure of how to charge customers, which can be more important than pricing levels
- **MUVs (Monthly Unique Visitors):** Metric Optimizely chose to charge by, bucketed into an experiment
- **Minivation:** Product created by bundling two products together without proper strategic consideration
- **Payer-rationalized trial design:** Trial approach designed to demonstrate value to payers, not just regulatory approval
- **Outside-in thinking:** Product development rooted in customer desires rather than internal product capabilities
- **Fuzzy front end:** Early stage of innovation when new product is often nebulous concept
- **Scope creep:** Situation in which list of features kept growing
- **Voice of the customer:** Direct feedback from customers about their needs and pain points
- **Test-sell:** Validating product ideas through customer research before starting product development
