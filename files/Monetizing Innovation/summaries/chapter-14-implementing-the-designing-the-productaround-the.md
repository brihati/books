# CHAPTER 14: Implementing the "Designing the Product Around the Price" Innovation Process

## Detailed Chapter Summary

Chapter 14 provides a comprehensive implementation framework for the nine rules of monetizing innovation through a structured, phased approach. The chapter emphasizes that knowing the principles is fundamentally different from executing them successfully—implementation requires understanding both mechanics and organizational barriers.

### Jump-Start and Pilot Phase

The implementation begins with diagnosis and piloting. Organizations should use an online diagnostic tool (available at http://www.monetizinginnovation.com) to assess current innovation process strengths and weaknesses, then review past product launches to categorize them as feature shock, minivation, hidden gem, undead, or big success. A representative new product is selected for the pilot phase, and a cross-functional team is formed with members from R&D, product, pricing, marketing, sales, and finance.

Critical to this phase is appointing a **monetization hero**—an individual with solid product experience and broad organizational perspective who will be accountable for the initiative's success and placed in charge of the team. Two rules of engagement are essential: (1) hold team members accountable for ideas they generate, and (2) empower people to say no to features that cause feature shock or undead products. This creates **constructive conflict**, where each team member challenges others' ideas to prevent the natural product developer tendency to believe "more is better," which contradicts the "less is more" principle required for monetization success.

During the pilot phase, organizations should adopt a "test and learn" mindset, survey target customers to understand what they value and their willingness to pay (WTP), and document what is and isn't working. Process steps should be captured in a workflow diagram for organizational clarity.

### Scale and Stick Phase

Scaling involves selecting one product from each primary product group to avoid a "big-bang" approach—the principle that "you need to crawl before you walk, and you need to walk before you run." A dedicated, full-time monetization team should be established that works across products and attends all innovation meetings. In startups, the monetization team and cross-functional innovation team can be one entity; in larger companies, a separate team focused solely on developing and applying the new process is preferable. Companies like Dräger and Swarovski have monetization teams that work closely with product teams daily, which has been instrumental to their success.

Performance must be tracked using sales, operations, and customer KPIs—not just financial metrics. Regular reviews should measure product performance against business plan predictions.

Creating lasting culture change requires comprehensive change management: training plans to fill skills gaps in product, marketing, and sales; documented change-management plans explaining why change is necessary and how to execute it; and leadership by example, with executives attending new-product meetings and asking tough questions. Success stories should be shared to convey the importance of monetizing innovation.

### Cultural Transformation

A fundamental cultural shift is required from a "yes/maybe/no" approval process to "no/maybe/yes." In traditional cultures, by the time organizations can say no, it is too late—the product has already launched. Instead, every product must earn its way into the market before launch, as exemplified by Porsche's approach. Organizations should create a list of the costliest undead products and kill them to free resources and establish the right culture for successful innovation.

Managers must be rewarded for developing products by enlisting customer input for design and pricing decisions, and discouraged from lowballing product goals and pricing. When managers lower prices without justification, they return profits that belong to the company to customers in the form of ill-conceived discounts. Best-in-class companies institute automated workflows with stage gates to ensure products pass one stage before moving to the next.

### Critical Success Factors

**C-suite leadership commitment is non-negotiable.** Companies where senior executives led change to extract full value from new products averaged 33 percent more profit than companies where executives delegated responsibility. If monetizing innovation is not one of the top two organizational priorities and reflected in C-level involvement, the organization should not embark on the journey.

The chapter presents a fundamental choice: continue with hope-based innovation (the "blue pill"—building products and hoping they deliver profit) or transition to data-driven, systematic monetization (the "red pill"—moving from hoping to knowing). Building products on wishes, hopes, and dreams is not sustainable in a world where innovation success is increasingly important and difficult.

- Implementation requires two sequential phases: Jump-Start and Pilot, then Scale and Stick
- A designated monetization hero with product experience and broad organizational perspective must lead and be held accountable
- Two critical rules of engagement: hold team members accountable for ideas and empower people to say no to features
- Constructive conflict and the ability to challenge features prevent feature shock and undead products
- Organizations must shift from "yes/maybe/no" to "no/maybe/yes" approval processes
- Dedicated, full-time monetization teams working across products are essential for larger organizations

- Killing undead products creates culture for successful innovation
- C-suite leadership commitment is critical; monetizing innovation must be a top two organizational priority

## Definitions and Terms

- **Monetization hero**: The individual accountable for the monetization initiative's success and placed in charge of the team
- **Constructive conflict**: A culture created when each team member holds others accountable for the ideas they generate
- **Feature shock**: An unsuccessful product launch resulting from including too many features
- **Undead products**: Products that continue to consume resources despite not being viable
- **Minivation**: A type of product launch outcome
- **Hidden gem**: A type of product launch outcome
- **Big success**: A type of product launch outcome
- **WTP (Willingness to Pay)**: The value customers place on a new offering
- **Blue pill**: Continuing with comfortable but fabricated reality; building products and hoping they deliver profit
- **Red pill**: Confronting difficult truth; transitioning from hoping to knowing through systematic monetization

## Actionable Lessons

**Diagnosis and Planning:**
- Choose a few recent products to diagnose and compare actual performance against business plan predictions
- Use the online diagnostic tool to assess innovation process strengths and weaknesses
- Review the nine rules and CEO question checklists from Chapters 4-12 with your team
- Attend early-stage R&D meetings tactfully and in advance to challenge and educate the new product team
- Explain your vision and goals for monetizing innovation to prepare the team

**Team Formation and Governance:**
- Select one representative product for the pilot phase if you are a multiproduct company
- Form a cross-functional team with members from R&D, product, pricing, marketing, sales, and finance
- Appoint a monetization hero with solid product experience and broad organizational perspective
- Establish two rules of engagement: hold team members accountable for ideas and empower them to say no
- Assign clear roles and responsibilities to cross-functional innovation teams
- Meet regularly and ask the right questions from CEO questions in Chapters 4-12

**Execution and Learning:**
- Survey target customers to get quantitative understanding of market size and customer value
- Document what is and isn't working during the pilot phase
- Put process steps into a workflow diagram that people can understand
- Adopt a "test and learn" mindset during the pilot phase as an iterative process
- Dedicate a full-time monetization team that works across products and attends all innovation meetings
- Track performance using sales, operations, and customer KPIs, not just financial KPIs
- Implement a review schedule to measure product performance

**Scaling and Culture Change:**
- Select one product from each primary product group to avoid big-bang approach
- Create a training plan to fill skills gaps in product, marketing, and sales
- Develop a change-management plan that documents why change is necessary, what needs to be done, and how to do it
- Lead by example by showing up at new-product meetings and asking tough questions
- Tell success stories to convey the importance of monetizing innovation
- Create a list of costliest undead products and kill them to create the right culture for innovation
- Shift organizational approval process from "yes/maybe/no" to "no/maybe/yes"
- Get every group equally excited, invested in, and motivated through incentives to embrace change
- Institute automated workflows with stage gates to ensure adherence to process
- Allocate sufficient budgets and adequate people to steps outlined in Chapters 4 through 12
- Reward people who develop products by enlisting customer input for design and pricing
- Discourage lowballing of new product goals and pricing

**Leadership and Commitment:**
- Ensure C-suite leadership is committed and monetizing innovation is a top two organizational priority
- Replace hope-based innovation processes with systematic, data-driven approaches
- Implement radical changes across product development, marketing, sales, and executive functions
- Build innovation strategy on reality and evidence rather than wishes and gut instinct
