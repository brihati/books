Simplicity 9 How can the rest of us achieve such enviable freedom from clutter? The answer is to clear our heads of clutter. Clear think- ing becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other. It's impossible for a muddy thinker to write good English. He may get away with it for a paragraph or two, but soon the reader will be lost, and there's no sin so grave, for the reader will not easily be lured back. Who is this elusive creature, the reader? The reader is some- one with an attention span of about 30 seconds—a person assailed by many forces competing for attention. At one time those forces were relatively few: newspapers, magazines, radio, spouse, chil- dren, pets. Today they also include a "home entertainment center" (television, VCR, tapes, CDs), e-mail, the Internet, the cellular phone, the fax machine, a fitness program, a pool, a lawn, and that most potent of competitors, sleep. The man or woman snoozing in a chair with a magazine or a book is a person who was being given too much unnecessary trouble by the writer. It won't do to say that the reader is too dumb or too lazy to keep pace with the train of thought. If the reader is lost, it's usu- ally because the writer hasn't been careful enough. That care- lessness can take any number of forms. Perhaps a sentence is so excessively cluttered that the reader, hacking through the ver- biage, simply doesn't know what it means.
