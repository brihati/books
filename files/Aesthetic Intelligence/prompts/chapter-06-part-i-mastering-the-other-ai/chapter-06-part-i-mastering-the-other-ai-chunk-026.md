There’s also a strong argument to be made that junk food is called that for a reason: worse than not being nutritious, it has a deleterious effect on our health if eaten consistently over time. I don’t think it’s going out on a limb to object to this use of aesthetics. Capitalists and entrepreneurs alike should have a conscience. Aesthetics is powerful, and if your business strategy is to use it for ill gain, it can backfire in terms of reputation (and commercially). Juul, a company now partially owned by the leading cigarette maker, Altria, is one example of this; in October 2018, the FDA conducted a surprise raid on the company’s San Francisco offices and

seized more than a thousand documents pertaining to its marketing, sales strategy, and product design specifically as it appeals to teenagers and other young people. 36 The seizure was done to make sure the company is complying with federal regulations regarding the product’s sales and marketing. It’s a concern, because e-cigarette use among teenagers has increased as conventional cigarette smoking has decreased. Nearly 12 percent of high school students and about 3 percent of middle school students used e-cigarettes in 2017, compared to about 7.6 percent of high schoolers who smoked regular cigarettes. Though Juul has been successful, we have to ask ourselves at what cost. And do you want your tween to take up the habit? Camel cigarettes and Joe Camel are the forerunners of Juul. Indeed, the upstart company seems to have taken a page directly from the R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Company’s confidential 1973 report entitled “Research Planning Memorandum on Some Thoughts About New Brands of Cigarettes for the Youth Market” by its marketing department’s Claude E. Teague, Jr. “Realistically, if our Company is to survive and prosper, over the long term, we must get our share of the youth market,” 37 he wrote. Teague also muses about ways to attract “presmokers” or “learners” to smoking in a marketplace in which the company cannot do so overtly: “It should be said that we are presently, and I believe unfairly, constrained from directly promoting cigarettes to the youth market. . . . In my opinion this will require new brands tailored to the youth market.” Teague goes on to discuss how Reynolds can exploit the psychological needs of youngsters by developing a new cigarette brand tailored to become the “in” brand; its promotion should emphasize togetherness, belonging, and group acceptance, while at the same time emphasizing individuality and “doing one’s own thing.” 38 Reynolds has continued to pursue the youth market through aesthetics and in various ways throughout the years. In 2004, it aggressively marketed candy-flavored cigarettes, which were obviously not intended to appeal to adult smokers. Partly as a result of this tactic, a 2009 federal law, the Family Smoking Prevention and Tobacco Control Act, banned candy- and fruit-flavored cigarettes. 39 In 2006, Reynolds, together with other tobacco companies, was found guilty of racketeering. Judge Gladys Kessler found it guilty of violating civil racketeering laws and engaging in a decades-long fraud to deceive the American public about the health risks of smoking and its marketing to children. Kessler highlighted the Joe Camel campaign in particular, finding that from the 1950s, the company had “intentionally marketed to young people under the age of twenty-one in order to recruit ‘replacement smokers’ to ensure the economic future of the tobacco industry.” 40 As late as 2013, Reynolds was back to placing ads in magazines read by many young people, including ESPN The Magazine, Sports Illustrated, and People. The ads were for a youth-targeted brand called Camel Crush. 41 POM Wonderful has also run afoul of the law with deceptive claims bolstered by its clever use of aesthetics. The juice is manufactured by a company owned by Lynda and Stewart Resnick, California billionaires who single- handedly created a multimillion-dollar market for pomegranate juice where there previously was none. Along with juice, the Resnicks created two pomegranate-based nutritional supplements, POMx pills and POMx liquid. The couple also spent $35 million to sponsor research so they could make claims about POM’s health benefits. 42 In 2010, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) filed a complaint against the Resnicks along with some of their business partners and two of their companies for deceptive trade practices, including misleading claims that the juice would prevent heart attacks, prostate cancer, and erectile dysfunction. In 2012, the FTC issued a cease-and-desist order, ruling that POM lacked credible scientific evidence that the juice and the dietary supplements could prevent any of the diseases they claimed to. 43 It turned out that POM Wonderful wasn’t so wonderful after all. In 2013, the Kellogg Company agreed to pay $4 million to settle a class action lawsuit over marketing claims it had made in 2009 for its Frosted Mini-Wheats, which were that consumption of the cereal increased children’s attention spans by almost 20 percent. 44 The FTC said the studies cited in the advertisement actually showed that the children who ate the cereal for breakfast averaged just under 11 percent greater attentiveness than children who ate no breakfast at all. Moreover, relatively few of the study’s participants reached anywhere near the 20 percent more attentive mark. Michael Moss wrote in his book Salt Sugar Fat: How the Food Giants Hooked Us that the clinical study Kellogg talked about in its ads (a study paid for by Kellogg) didn’t support even the most minimal claims. “The truly remarkable aspect of the campaign is that the company study, even if taken at face value, did not come close to supporting the claim in its advertising. Half of the children who ate bowls of Frosted Minis showed no improvement at all on the tests they were given to measure their ability to remember, think, and reason, as compared with their ability before eating the cereal.” 45 Kellogg wanted to create an “aesthetic of health” around what is basically a sugary and carb-heavy cereal—and surprisingly, like the Resnicks in their claims about POM Wonderful, failed to appreciate that most false claims are eventually exposed and undermine the brand’s integrity. In the long run, true aesthetic value can only be built on pure intentions and defensible actions.
