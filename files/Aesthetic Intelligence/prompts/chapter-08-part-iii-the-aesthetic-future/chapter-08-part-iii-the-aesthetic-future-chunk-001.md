Part III The Aesthetic Future Chapter 9 The Future of Aesthetics Increasingly, we seem to be living in two worlds: one dominated by automation, algorithms, and attention deprivation; the other in search of human-centered interactions, emotional connection, and experiences designed specifically for us as individuals. My auto mechanic, tax accountant, and deliveryman may soon be replaced by computer-based services or digital beings, while my hairdresser, massage therapist, and interior decorator certainly won’t be (at least not for a long while). This split will affect aesthetics, which will also continue to evolve. Cultural and demographic shifts will of course continue to influence what we deem to be beautiful or pleasing and what we reject as unattractive and undesirable. As we have already seen with the rise of social media, human activity will continue to focus on what I call REM: relationships, experiences, and memories. Our desire to connect with other people intimately, authentically, and personally may result in a rejection of some forms of social media 1 and usher in new forms of community building, signaled by millennials’ and others’ migration out of so-called superstar cities such as New York and Los Angeles into smaller towns and cities. 2 “In the last couple of years, we’ve seen the rise of the exodus of big cities,” says Steven Pedigo, an economic and urban development expert and director of the New York University Schack Institute of Real Estate Urban Lab. “Places are taking the ideas of what people want in urban communities, and smaller and suburban communities are trying to recreate this.” 3 This migration may be prompted by economics (large cities are expensive to live in) and technological advances that enable people to work outside large urban centers, but newly populated small towns and cities also will flourish due to human-driven creative reasons. Aesthetics, not automation, will continue to support and promote the growth of these creative communities. That means people everywhere—not just in large urban centers—will seek out and expect to find a high level of aesthetics in the goods and services they want and need. If they cannot find them, they will make them. Many entrepreneurs will start companies with strong and clear aesthetic values. If existing companies can unleash their employees’ aesthetic intelligence and capabilities, they will be able to provide the holistic and humanistic experiences that more and more people will want, expect, and demand. Other social and cultural shifts support this belief and will affect where business and aesthetics will go over the next fifteen to twenty years. Here’s my take on four major trends that will redefine the aesthetics of business. As you will see, they are inextricably interrelated and interdependent. The Environmental Crisis Consumers realize that they can no longer afford to be complacent about the environment. One of the ways we can practice environmental stewardship and responsibility is by paying attention to the products we buy—using our economic might to drive change and make the world better or at least less toxic. One study on corporate social responsibility (CSR), conducted by Cone/Porter Novelli, found that consumers do care how products are made. 4 A few key takeaways from the study include: 87 percent of consumers say they have a more positive image of companies that support social or environmental issues than those that don’t. 88 percent of respondents said they show more loyalty to companies that support environmental issues than those that don’t. 87 percent would buy products with environmental benefits if given that option. 92 percent are more likely to trust companies that support environmental issues. Of all groups surveyed, millennials were the most likely to use word of mouth and social media to share information about companies they feel are environmentally and socially responsible—as well as those that they think are not in step with environmental realities. As millennials become an increasingly dominant economic group, companies must be prepared to ensure, promote, and support their environmental impact. And they must do so credibly, as millennials are skeptical of any outlandish claims. Aesthetics can play a key part in this effort by creating a clear and distinct narrative around a company’s eco-friendly manufacturing policies and practices, which may include the innovative use of recyclable or reusable packaging. The international food giant Nestlé announced in April 2018 that it would make all of its packaging either recyclable or reusable by 2025. 5 Amcor, Ecover, Evian, L’Oréal, Mars, Marks & Spencer, PepsiCo, the Coca-Cola Company, Unilever, Walmart, and Werner & Mertz have made similar promises. 6 The dairy producer Organic Valley’s packaging is already recyclable (or reusable). 7 The sportswear company Patagonia calls itself an “activist company” and says it is in business specifically to help the environment. 8 The household products company Seventh Generation similarly sees itself as being on a social and environmental mission. 9 I believe there will be renewed interest in using brighter, bolder colors on products and other visual cues that are inspired by or reflective of nature; a preference for textures and materials that feel natural (e.g., less use of polyester and other blends in clothing manufacturing); a desire to see references or indications that store-bought items are handmade; and an acceptance of and even attraction to imperfect, inconsistent production (à la the Japanese philosophy wabi-sabi). Big retailers have already begun to experiment with bringing “small-batch” artisanal items into their stores on a regional basis. The home furnishings retailer West Elm, a subsidiary of Williams-Sonoma, sells products from about five hundred craftspeople 10 and has brought in “significant revenues” by doing so. 11 Karen Guethlein, a Trion, Georgia, ceramist, 12 has made dishes, bowls, and plates for major retailers, including Whole Foods and Anthropologie. In 2016, Etsy established a partnership with Macy’s 13 that features a revolving group of Etsy artisan goods, including meet-and-greet events with the makers. As these makers find ways to scale production to meet larger retail demands while still maintaining the authenticity of their handmade goods, more of their products will be found at larger retailers. Expect more social and environmental activism, greater efforts toward sustainability, and further inclusion of handmade items as a response to consumer interest in products with a small environmental impact. This will in turn lead to a desire for more tactile experiences.
