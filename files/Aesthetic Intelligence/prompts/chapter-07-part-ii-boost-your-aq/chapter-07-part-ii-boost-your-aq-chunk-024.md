Still, no other scooter has the same prestige, reputation, or history as a Vespa. If you watch any Italian movie from the 1950s onward, you will see characters riding around on Vespas. In fact, if you go to Rome or any other Italian city, you will see rows of Vespas parked neatly next to sidewalks. Not only are they beautiful, they are highly functional when it comes to maneuvering in narrow city streets. This use in both popular culture and real life has welded itself to the collective unconscious. Vespas connote freedom, urbanity, sophistication, style, and fun. Part of the scooters’ charm is due to their basically unchanged but seductive design. They look pretty much the same as they always have. The 1946 design was streamlined, and although the scooter looks somewhat retro today, it doesn’t look kitschy or old-fashioned. It’s also made of metal, whereas competitors have long since traded in that costly material for cheap plastic components. They are, simply put, very beautiful objects built to last. Vespa construction has what is called a monocoque frame, meaning the bodywork is the frame. Most other scooters have separate body panels attached to a frame. This construction makes it both lighter and more rigid. As a result, the ride is very smooth, another appealing quality, especially when navigating urban asphalt and cobblestones. Vespa understands what it is—the words it chooses to describe its history and its brand echo the feeling people have when they ride on one: “young,” “freedom,” “passion,” “beautiful,” and “future.” 30 It also continues to innovate where innovation will actually make a difference to consumers. Vespa’s Elettrica model deploys advanced connectivity, silent operation, customization, and sustainability. 31 Its greatest articulation, however, may come from the owners themselves, who have a strong sense of pride of ownership, a pride they are enthusiastic about sharing through word of mouth, in Vespa meet-ups, and on social media (there are more than five million results for #Vespa on Instagram, and only about three million for the generic #scooter). Who Stands to Gain? Beyond making a case for the value of good design is a plea for a more serious consideration of and conversation about who we intend to delight and inspire with our products. Ethical concerns have everything to do with articulation. It makes sense to think deeply about how we do what we do and how we communicate it because consumers want to know. And they are growing impatient with companies that don’t care about them. About 92 percent of millennials say they are more likely to buy products from ethical companies, according to research by the insurance company Aflac. 32 Part of a brand’s ethical obligation to consumers (and to the planet) is to communicate how their products benefit not just the buyer but the “greater good”—whether that is the environment or other social causes. This effort is going to become increasingly important as we move from consumerism to a postconsumer society. Consumerism took off after World War II and has dominated our economic landscape since at least the 1970s, when the primary economic activity of the average person shifted from saving and frugality to spending and indulging in goods and services. As I indicated in the beginning of this book, after decades of unremitting dominance, consumerism, as a lifestyle, is starting to erode; in many circles, it is greeted with suspicion and contempt. The popularity of the minimalist movement is one such indicator, as is the sharing economy, and the growth of experience-based businesses that cater to people’s desire to create moments and memories that will last a lifetime. I welcome this shift. We do have too much stuff, and much of the stuff we have is devoid of meaning, durability, and workmanship. As we move into a postconsumer society, ethical marketing must be more than a public relations strategy; it has to underpin everything we do as brands and producers. In other words, it must be operationalized in and integrated into every aspect of the business. That means marketing and advertising must be honest and deserving of trust and attention, product packaging must be creative and ethical, and brand values must inspire and delight. I’m not alone in making this prediction; it’s a view shared by many of my colleagues involved in product innovation and production, economists, and others. 33 In the final chapter, I go a step further and talk about broad trends that I believe will shape the future of aesthetics in business. I point to just a few powerful movements already under way. Others will emerge, and I encourage you to keep an eye out for them and to make your own connections between external trends and the effect they have on your own sources of delight and desire.
