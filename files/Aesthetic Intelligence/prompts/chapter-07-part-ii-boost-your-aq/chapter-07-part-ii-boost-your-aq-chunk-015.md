As digital retailers such as Amazon, Wayfair, and others continue to develop and perfect algorithms that prioritize convenience and predictive buying choices for consumers, the pressure is on bricks-and-mortar department stores to curate not only their product offerings but also the experiences they offer customers. Fortunately, there are still ways for department stores (and other bricks- and-mortar retailers) to thrive: by offering customers compelling reasons to enter their physical spaces and by inspiring them to spend money, including offering fewer but better choices. They also need to convey a stronger point

of view about who they are and what they stand for (and what kinds of customers they want to cultivate). Putting forth a distinctive point of view won’t please everyone—that’s not the point—but it will resonate with their most loyal customers. Aesthetically minded retailers must also provide excellent service. They need to invest in hiring and developing staff who genuinely care about serving other people and are equipped with knowledge and know-how. All this grows out of the intention and the necessity to create a deeply immersive and elevated experience that cannot be easily replicated by other stores and certainly cannot be replicated online. Offline retailers must also be nimbler and find ways to bring novelty and surprise to shoppers. To do so requires radical curatorial intervention and the difficult proposition of throwing out old metrics of success, such as sales per square foot, retail conversion rates, and average sales per order 6 in favor of more meaningful metrics, such as length, engagement, and memorability of in- store experience and the association with purchase decisions, product satisfaction, and propensity to come back. Some stores are already experimenting with the “fresh is best” approach. Consider, for instance, Chicago-area Carson’s department store’s new owner, Justin Yoshimura, a technology entrepreneur who bought the intellectual property of Bon-Ton Holdings, which included Carson’s, for $900,000 in September 2018. Under Yoshimura’s leadership, Carson’s no longer restocks departments once a season. Instead, the store receives new merchandise every day and changes all inventory every two weeks. That means if you see it and you want it, you must buy it right then, because it’s likely not going to be there the next time you shop the store. But the next time you go to Carson’s, you’re going to see lots of new things. 7 Carson’s has also decreased the size of its clothing department by 50 percent but expanded the size of its toy and furniture departments—categories customers have indicated they are more interested in buying in a store as opposed to online. This makes sense, as customers may want to check out the quality and safety of toys and the look and comfort of large-scale furniture before committing to buying. Curating Experiences Changing merchandise frequently and reducing options are two promising strategies for retail success. Another is creating a seductive environment that delivers entertainment and enlightenment. Three of my favorite offline stores are 10 Corso Como, 8 Dover Street Market, 9 and ABC Carpet & Home. The first two have select locations around the world; the third is based in New York City. 10 Each has succeeded through mindful curation. They sell many of the same categories of products and brands as larger stores such as Bloomingdale’s and Barneys New York, but they do so in a way that makes shopping fun, exciting, memorable, and desirable. And because they’ve curated their offerings based on certain sensibilities— rather than trying to make their offerings encyclopedic as online stores can do or exhaustive as traditional department stores have done—they make it easier for customers to choose. They don’t offer everything for everyone; they focus on a distinct kind of customer and give them only the very best selection of choices. Interestingly, Bloomingdale’s in the 1980s and Barneys in the 1990s offered similarly exciting shopping experiences, but neither store has managed to maintain the qualities that originally made it a “destination”— such as special merchandise and fanciful displays—decades ago. With locations in Milan, Seoul, Beijing, Shanghai, and New York, 10 Corso Como was founded in 1990 by former Vogue Italia fashion editor Carla Sozzani as what she calls a “virtual narrative,” with an art gallery and bookstore at the core. It feels very much like a living, breathing magazine with discernable editorial choices, or curation, in food, fashion, art, music, lifestyle, and design. Shoppers or visitors learn, understand, and see objects in context. Customers engage with products in the way they might do so at home—touching, holding, and trying on are encouraged. The curation of the goods offered is also unique: international, often artisanal, and handmade. You won’t see the same things offered in other department stores. So not only is the experience of walking through the shop special and fun, but you won’t be able to procure the same items on Amazon using your phone—avoiding the so-called showrooming effect that has hurt traditional retailers in the last few years. Moreover, all this aesthetic wonderment is accomplished in about twenty-five thousand square feet, which is about 20 percent the size of a typical department store. Dover Street Market similarly presents brands and ideas in a narrative fashion. Its displays are lively and inventive. They tell a story about the product, its designer—and the potential customer. Founder Rei Kawakubo told reporters, “I want to create a kind of market where various creators from various fields gather together and encounter each other in an ongoing atmosphere of beautiful chaos, the mixing up and coming together of different kindred souls who all share a strong personal vision.” 11 In one area of the London store, hats are perched on a pile of banquet chairs placed atop one another in a sculptural and almost treelike effect. You pluck a hat off one of the chair “branches” to try it on. Its Nike Shop, a shop within the shop, is uniquely organized and displayed. Of course, you could buy your Nike workout clothes online, but Dover Street Market’s ingenuity entices customers to make their purchases then and there. That it can double as an event space makes “shopping Nike” even more experiential. Dover Street Market breaks a lot of retail rules concerned with display. It uses many unexpected display strategies, such as piling hats on a random pile of chairs or creating pathways through various display structures that combine stacking and hung merchandise, which is unlike the conventional method of aisles created by racks of clothing that is ubiquitous in most department stores. The result is a unique exploratory experience combined with a distinct set of offerings that reflect both the store’s aesthetics and its customers’ aspirations and their thirst for novelty and surprise over convenience and “friction-free” shopping. ABC Carpet & Home is a much bigger space than both 10 Corso Como and Dover Street Market, but what is interesting about this Manhattan institution is that despite its size and breadth of product offerings, the store still maintains a tight focus and commitment to whom and what it represents. I often compare ABC’s flagship store to the inside of a genie’s bottle, with its piles of brightly colored silk rugs, stacks of decorative pillows, clash of vintage and contemporary furniture, and unexpected finds.
