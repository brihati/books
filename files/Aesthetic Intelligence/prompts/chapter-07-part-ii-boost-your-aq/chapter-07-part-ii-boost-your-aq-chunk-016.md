Everything Is Personal The process of curating your personal space will help prepare you to make better curation decisions in your business. Like all muscles, curatorial skills are developed through exercise. Moreover, once you’ve developed a strong sense of personal style—clarity and precision as to what feels good and does not feel good in your own life—you can apply that understanding and discernment to your business. You can establish a great deal of trust with your customers with the right curation. When we design and curate a home, office, retail space, or product, we must keep the user in mind. As I’ve said, the more you understand how you use a space (or how you experience food or wear clothes), the more empathy you can build for others. In interior design, we have to think about who is occupying the space and how they will use it—which forces us, if we really make a commitment to it, to curate the design elements and objects that we put into a space and how we place them. How do we like to live and feel in a space? Don’t get too serious; there’s nothing more uncomfortable than a stark and tense space. Humor brings relief; it’s relaxing and helps build connections. It’s an important part of conveying many kinds of messages—especially in design, including sophisticated design. Jonathan Adler built a business around this idea, incorporating whimsical figurines and ironic motifs into his offerings. Or consider Gulla Jónsdóttir, a Los Angeles–based architect and furniture designer originally from Iceland. She is especially skilled at designing spaces and furniture that work in harmony with their environmental surroundings. Her growing up in a rugged and majestic Nordic region and her connection to the distinctive natural elements of Iceland enabled interesting and empathetic spatial experiences that correspond to nature. Through the application of organic shapes and curves, natural materials such as marble, and neutral colors, her designs pay tribute to Iceland’s jagged mountains, black lava, misty gray fog, and dreamy seascapes. But Jónsdóttir also trained as a mathematician, so her designs feel precise and even geometrical, reflecting her sense of how people move through and use spaces. The rooms and spaces she designs are welcoming and relaxed but specific—that is, specifically designed for uses and moods. The work of “business aestheticians” such as Jónsdóttir demonstrates why business aesthetics must be an intentional, strategic practice even if the starting point is personal and intuitive.
