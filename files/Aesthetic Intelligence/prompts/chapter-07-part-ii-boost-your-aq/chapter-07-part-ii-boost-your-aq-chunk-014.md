Do not underestimate the power of aesthetics to help deliver that comfort and reassurance. When I visited Carlyle’s headquarters in Washington, DC, where cofounder and co–executive chairman David Rubenstein still presides, 4 I experienced a very specific set of values, as dictated by David and his cofounders. David came from a humble background (his father was a postal worker).

Even though he is now one of the wealthiest people in the United States, his fortune was self-made. Carlyle’s headquarters reflected David’s origins in many ways: simple, unadorned, and humble. The offices were small and had a makeshift look, as if a wall had been put up at random every time the company needed to add a desk for a new employee. Carlyle was not investing its considerable resources in space design or office furniture. Its focus on function and simplicity conveyed a critical value of Carlyle’s founders: that they did not care to invest in anything other than that which would make money for the firm’s investors and employees. This is an aesthetic—and it is not a right or wrong one. It does tell a potential partner or investor an important story: that this company is focused on the work and only on the work. The firm doesn’t signal elitism or luxury, even though it is staffed by some of the smartest and richest people in the world. To some, this bare-bones aesthetic is an important asset, one reason Carlyle is one of the most successful and trusted private equity firms in the United States. Contrast this to another legendary private equity firm, Henry Kravis’s KKR offices at 9 West 57th Street in Manhattan. Visitors must pass through layers of security even before they reach the reception area. The dark, wood-paneled walls are austere, fortresslike, and ostentatiously covered with precious art; the offices are decorated with heavy furniture. In contrast, when I visit the same executives’ homes, I experience something quite different: their homes are carefully decorated, with art on the walls, pillows on the couch, and furnishings acquired through different life stages and experiences. If the executive is a married man, it is often his wife who took the lead in their home design. But why does the expression of their home life have to be so different from that of their office life? My hunch is that many business executives view their homes as feminine spaces and their offices as masculine spaces. It’s an unnatural division, because even in the workplace, we are still human; pretending otherwise can undermine creative problem-solving and emotional well-being and hurt our business interests. It inhibits us from connecting authentically with one another and our customers. Curation, Choice, and the Demise (and Rebirth) of the Department Store Department store owners have always tried to design their spaces with customers in mind. More recently, however, the traditional department store aesthetic has lost its edge. The retail format has been in decline for decades —its share of retail sales fell from 5.54 percent in 1998 to 1.58 percent in 2017, according to the US Census Bureau 5—making redesign of its shopping experience a business imperative. These days consumers aren’t likely to view a visit to their local department store as a treasure hunt. They aren’t interested in lingering and browsing; they don’t luxuriate in the process of discovery and surprise. They want what they want now, and they don’t have much tolerance for long waits or news that their size is out of stock. The idea is to get what they want and get out. Old models of curation and traditional customer service hold little weight with them. As digital retailers such as Amazon, Wayfair, and others continue to develop and perfect algorithms that prioritize convenience and predictive buying choices for consumers, the pressure is on bricks-and-mortar department stores to curate not only their product offerings but also the experiences they offer customers.
