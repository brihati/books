Chapter 9
The Future of Aesthetics
Increasingly, we seem to be living in two worlds: one dominated by
automation, algorithms, and attention deprivation; the other in search of
human-centered interactions, emotional connection, and experiences
designed specifically for us as individuals. My auto mechanic, tax
accountant, and deliveryman may soon be replaced by computer-based
services or digital beings, while my hairdresser, massage therapist, and
interior decorator certainly won’t be (at least not for a long while). This
split will affect aesthetics, which will also continue to evolve. Cultural and
demographic shifts will of course continue to influence what we deem to be
beautiful or pleasing and what we reject as unattractive and undesirable. As
we have already seen with the rise of social media, human activity will
continue to focus on what I call REM: relationships, experiences, and
memories.
Our desire to connect with other people intimately, authentically, and
personally may result in a rejection of some forms of social media
1 and
usher in new forms of community building, signaled by millennials’ and
others’ migration out of so-called superstar cities such as New York and Los
Angeles into smaller towns and cities.
2 “In the last couple of years, we’ve
seen the rise of the exodus of big cities,” says Steven Pedigo, an economic
and urban development expert and director of the New York University
Schack Institute of Real Estate Urban Lab. “Places are taking the ideas of
what people want in urban communities, and smaller and suburban
communities are trying to recreate this.”
3
This migration may be prompted by economics (large cities are
expensive to live in) and technological advances that enable people to work
outside large urban centers, but newly populated small towns and cities also
will flourish due to human-driven creative reasons. Aesthetics, not
automation, will continue to support and promote the growth of these
creative communities. That means people everywhere—not just in large
urban centers—will seek out and expect to find a high level of aesthetics in
the goods and services they want and need. If they cannot find them, they
will make them. Many entrepreneurs will start companies with strong and
clear aesthetic values. If existing companies can unleash their employees’
aesthetic intelligence and capabilities, they will be able to provide the
holistic and humanistic experiences that more and more people will want,
expect, and demand.
Other social and cultural shifts support this belief and will affect where
business and aesthetics will go over the next fifteen to twenty years. Here’s
my take on four major trends that will redefine the aesthetics of business.
As you will see, they are inextricably interrelated and interdependent.
The Environmental Crisis
Consumers realize that they can no longer afford to be complacent about the
environment. One of the ways we can practice environmental stewardship
and responsibility is by paying attention to the products we buy—using our
economic might to drive change and make the world better or at least less
toxic. One study on corporate social responsibility (CSR), conducted by
Cone/Porter Novelli, found that consumers do care how products are made.
4
A few key takeaways from the study include:
87 percent of consumers say they have a more positive image of
companies that support social or environmental issues than those
that don’t.
88 percent of respondents said they show more loyalty to
companies that support environmental issues than those that don’t.
87 percent would buy products with environmental benefits if given
that option.
92 percent are more likely to trust companies that support
environmental issues.
Of all groups surveyed, millennials were the most likely to use word of
mouth and social media to share information about companies they feel are
environmentally and socially responsible—as well as those that they think
are not in step with environmental realities. As millennials become an
increasingly dominant economic group, companies must be prepared to
ensure, promote, and support their environmental impact. And they must do
so credibly, as millennials are skeptical of any outlandish claims.
Aesthetics can play a key part in this effort by creating a clear and
distinct narrative around a company’s eco-friendly manufacturing policies
and practices, which may include the innovative use of recyclable or
reusable packaging. The international food giant Nestlé announced in April
2018 that it would make all of its packaging either recyclable or reusable by
2025.

made similar promises.

I believe there will be renewed interest in using brighter, bolder colors on
products and other visual cues that are inspired by or reflective of nature; a
preference for textures and materials that feel natural (e.g., less use of
polyester and other blends in clothing manufacturing); a desire to see
references or indications that store-bought items are handmade; and an
acceptance of and even attraction to imperfect, inconsistent production (à la
the Japanese philosophy wabi-sabi). Big retailers have already begun to
experiment with bringing “small-batch” artisanal items into their stores on a
regional basis. The home furnishings retailer West Elm, a subsidiary of
Williams-Sonoma, sells products from about five hundred craftspeople
10 and
has brought in “significant revenues” by doing so.

Expect more social and environmental activism, greater efforts toward
sustainability, and further inclusion of handmade items as a response to
consumer interest in products with a small environmental impact. This will
in turn lead to a desire for more tactile experiences.
Digital Expansion and the Tactile Experience
The expansion and pervasiveness of advanced computing and “smart”
devices; the increasing automation of cars, houses, and large parts of the
workforce;
14 and cheaper, faster access to data—all are the result of a forty-
year trend that will continue for another forty years or more. Some people
will embrace high-tech experiences and products, while others will reject it,
leading to a new twist in the concept of a “digital divide.” We are entering a
world not of “haves versus have-nots” but of “wants versus don’t-wants.”
Automation will replace work in many sectors—farm,
15 fast food,
16 driving,
17
clerical, and other jobs
18 will be the first to go
19—but new jobs will be
created in industries that require creativity, originality, and a human touch
(literally and figuratively), including in the arts, sciences, and business
strategy,
20 which is why aesthetic intelligence is so important to the future of
work. If you do not have aesthetic skills, the digital world and the
handmade world may both seem out of reach. The caveat is that computers
can and will create art and music—but humans, I believe, will continue to
do so in much more elevated and inspiring ways. There may also be
something akin to “human privilege,” where many people will prefer—and
even pay more for—creative materials built by human minds and hands.
Jobs involving building and sustaining complex interpersonal relations,
including professions such as nursing, sports coaching, and psychotherapy,
will be somewhat safe from automation. Here, too, aesthetic intelligence
will be required to help services sustain and grow their client base, since
competition in these fields will be brisk.
Increased automation and computer learning also will compel people to
look for ever more creative and personal ways to improve quality of life.
With this will come a quest for items that have greater tactile qualities that
give us sensorial pleasure and relieve us of near-constant exposure to the
flatness of two-dimensional screens. A desire for richer, lusher sound will
push technology companies to create more lifelike listening experiences—
and we may also crave more live music experiences.
21 There will be an extra
appreciation for digital products that provide intensified scent/taste/touch
experiences and for nondigital products that provide a rich sensorial
experience as well. In fashion and clothing, sensory experiences may
indeed be woven into fabrics: think thick, chunky knits or those with
ultrasoft or nubby textures or mixed-media textiles (e.g., leather plus quilted
down-filled fabric plus embroidery).
In food, unusual and unexpected ingredients (e.g., spicy or savory ice
cream, even more intense sweet, hot, and sour flavors) will continue to push
the limits of culinary innovation, but we will also see a return to “comfort
foods” and those that offer warm, nostalgic feelings. Though some people
will opt for space-age foods such as Soylent, most of us want to experience
a variety of sensations and novelty when we gather for a meal.
All the while, technology will continue to evolve and become part of
high-tech fitness clothing and other wearables, keeping track of our steps,
body mass index (BMI), calories eaten and expended, blood pressure, and
so on. Tech will also affect food and drink, which will lead to more
functional foods that promise to enhance our health and/or mood. The
Hudson Valley–based Recess is a harbinger of this trend. Its water is
infused with a nonintoxicating hemp extract that claims to have pain
relieving, antianxiety, anti-inflammatory properties—it helps you “chill
out” without getting stoned. The drink contains adaptogens that purport to
reduce stress and improve memory, focus, and immunity.
22
There will be more emphasis on hands-on body work to achieve physical
fitness (as opposed to high-tech workouts), including massage and new
forms of yoga and other mind-body practices that intensify the experience
of movement. Death metal yoga is a good example of this, with classes that
feature punching, kicking, air guitar playing, head banging, and a great deal
of sweat.
23 Fitness centers may also become smaller, built closer to patrons
even in rural areas, and more personalized or niche oriented. That means
small centers that serve older populations or younger ones; those that serve
transgender people or specific religious groups.
24
Focusing on small communities and their needs is a way to be
competitive in a crowded field, not just for the fitness business but for most
businesses. Niche markets that serve various age groups and desires will
become more common, and their aesthetic choices will differentiate them.
To offset a depersonalized society, consumers will crave recognition of their
individuality, which will lead to the next shift.
Tribal Secession
When I use the word secession, I don’t mean it in the sense that countries
will break up into smaller ones—although that could happen, and many
geopolitical experts and others have predicted it will, as exemplified by
Brexit.
25 However, as a reaction to globalization and threats to local cultures,
languages, and ways of life, we have seen a rapid growth of identity
politics, tribalism, localism, activism, and unfortunately terrorism. More
than ever, people seek to belong to groups that represent mutual feelings,
shared values and common goals, and joint causes or ideals that validate
their belief systems. These forces will be fed by social media and may
undermine both democracies and dictatorships.
26
The growth of “tribes” will usher in an age of hyperlocalism (and a
rejection of global harmonization) and the emergence of “microdominant”
brands that cater to highly specific cultures and identities, defined not
necessarily by ethnic background but by personal values and lifestyle
choices. Brands that serve microcommunities (e.g., trans or gender-fluid
people, religious factions, historically underserved and overlooked groups,
and so on) will redefine retail by creating authenticity, integrity, and
transformative products and experiences that consumers want but can’t
always find right now. Tribalism is the most powerful force on earth today.
Communities become tribes. Brands form tribes. Large companies are tribes
of tribes.
For business that means we will see two consumer desires played out
simultaneously. First, we will see products that appeal to smaller and more
specific group identities. We will also see products that convey a sense of
global and blended design influenced by access to a range of cultural
influences. The mixing and blending of cultural heritages will create new
hybrid groups and identities, such as “tribal tech” and “industrial chic.”
People will form groups or “tribes” in other ways as a reaction to the fear of
the harsh, unpredictable realities of the outside world. Cocooning will
continue to be important, along with the accoutrements that support it—
everything from cozy blankets to products and services that bring us closer
to safety and elicit trust and comfort.
Anxiety-ridden by simultaneous social, economic, political, and ethical
chaos, some people will find themselves stretched beyond their ability to
cope with today or imagine tomorrow. The search for purpose, meaning,
and spiritual connection will become even more pronounced. The search to
find like-minded people, especially those that offer spiritual and emotional
sustenance and protection from unfriendly forces and persecution (real or
imagined), will open up new realms for aesthetic innovation as well. New
products and services that acknowledge and celebrate religious faith and
spirituality will find new and nontraditional markets for their offerings.
Venxara’s Spiritual Warriors Couture
27 line exemplifies this, with its
handbags, totes, and jewelry adorned with artwork featuring various
Catholic saints. You’ll also see a growth in modest fashion prompted by and
geared toward women of Muslim, Orthodox Jewish, and other religious
faiths. The new modesty will find its way into mainstream fashion, and
many people will gravitate toward it for the same reasons they will gravitate
toward spirituality—for a sense of comfort and protection from the hostile
outside world.
As communities focus on spiritual growth and renewal, expect a sharper
focus on purpose, meaning, and making a positive difference. Expect
intense focus on long-term sustainability of our planet.
There will also be nostalgia for earlier generations’ seemingly carefree
childhoods. Baby boomers will find comfort in familiar pursuits and
products from their youth, as will millennials and Gen Xers. Retrograde
brands such as Pabst and Schlitz (beer), Polaroid (cameras), Schwinn
(bicycles), Keds (sneakers), Fresca (soda), Shalimar (perfume), Arthur
Treacher’s Fish & Chips (fast food), Hydrox (cookies), and Fiorucci
(fashion) will be back in demand. New brands that can successfully evoke
nostalgic feelings of a more innocent and insular time also will win.
Secession into tribes or cultural/value groups will affect the beauty
industry as well. There will be a shift from the idealization of youth to a
focus on healthy, happy, intelligent aging. This is happening already,
through recent advertisements from the beauty chain store Ulta Beauty. In
2016 and through part of 2018, Ulta’s television and print ads had a
cartoonlike quality, presenting women as perfect, doll-like beings. Despite
there being a variety of ethnicities represented, they all looked pretty much
the same. Today, Ulta’s branding and marketing celebrate beauty that comes
in all shapes and sizes, a welcome message that is helping the company
succeed in a variety of markets. It’s also one of the few retailers to sell
high-end and low-end products side by side.

Blurred Lines
As I said earlier, people will form groups driven by shared ideologies,
interests, and beliefs, but oftentimes groups and group members will
identify outside of conventional norms. There is already a blurring of lines
happening between male and female; straight and gay; black and white; and
young and old.
30 As a result, more brands and categories once
conventionally divided by gender or age will become unisex or offer unisex
items and age-fluid products and services. The children’s brand Primary
31
offers basics such as T-shirts, leggings, pants, skirts, and dresses in bright
solid colors meant to be worn by all children from ages zero to twelve;
conventional boys (pants, Ts) and girls (dresses, skirts) items are marketed
to all children. The Phluid Project in SoHo in New York City may be the
first officially gender-free retail space in the world. At three thousand
square feet, the bright white store with large windows and high ceilings is
part retail space and part “experiential platform,” according to its content
director, Jillian Brooks.
32
The store, aimed toward gender-nonconforming and gender-fluid
consumers, uses customized gender-free mannequins that show off unisex
basics from brands such as Levi’s and Soulland, along with more fashion-
forward choices from Gypsy Sport, Skingraft, and the fetish-inspired latex
by Meat. The Phluid Project
33 also offers its own line of T-shirts and
hoodies, adorned with slogans including “Stronger together” and “One
world.” Part of its mission is affordability, so prices are generally less than
$300. No Sesso (“no sex/gender” in Italian) is another brand that has taken
the idea of genderless clothing to new and unique areas with its use of
vibrant color combinations; methods of tying, stitching, and embroidery;
irregular knitting; and billowing or highly tailored fabrics. The clothes work
with various body shapes (male/female, short/tall, large/small) because they
have convertible or transformable features.
34 In other words, customers can
customize the clothing in many ways to suit their shape and identity.
TomboyX is the anti–Victoria’s Secret, a company with an explicit
agenda
35 that, although it purports to be “human focused,” is made by and
for lesbians. Expect to see existing brands adding similar lines to their
offerings along with an onslaught of new brands geared toward various
sexually and personality-driven identities. At the same time, we’ll see new
brands and services that present hyped-up, ironic versions of the
stereotypical distinctions between male/female. You’ll see exaggerations
such as larger-than-life puffy sleeves and other hyperfeminine details such
as ruffles, covered buttons, and other frills on shirts and blouses.

Forging positive human connections is a complex effort and has far-
reaching implications. If done well, it can lead to richer brand experiences.
But the onus is on creators to align their ideas with motives worthy of being
experienced personally and profoundly. The modern consumer, no longer
driven to accumulate material possessions, seeks depth, authenticity, and
meaning. Therefore, the brands that will endure will provide purpose—one
that extends well beyond commercial motives and that unites and empowers
the people touched by their products or services. In the end, that is what
truly and eternally challenges, compels, and delights their consumers—any
and all opportunities to care for and respect them not for their consumption
but for their humanity.
