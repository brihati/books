# Chapter 05: Step 2 - Positioning & Packaging

## Detailed Chapter Summary

### Understanding Positioning

Positioning is fundamentally about carving a place of influence in a buyer's mind, not about what you do to the product itself. It involves differentiating your brand through an oversimplified message designed to cut through communication clutter. Positioning directly impacts pricing strategy because pricing cannot be set without clear positioning first.

Heuristic thinking—described as "muscle memory for the brain"—enables micro-second preferences and biases in buyer decisions. Buyers compare offerings against prominent competitors they recognize and use three key heuristics: vendors they can relate to, what similar peers think, and thought leaders they follow. Once positioning is established in the buyer's mind, it creates perceived differentiation even when actual product differences may not exist, enabling premium pricing through established heuristic biases.

The Lucky Strike cigarette example illustrates this principle: the "It's Toasted" positioning created a perceived differentiator despite all cigarettes using toasting. First-mover advantage in positioning cannot be replicated by competitors without appearing as an emulator, giving early positioning a lasting competitive edge.

### Three Essential Positioning Questions

Most pricing problems are not actually pricing problems but results of poorly understood positioning. Before any pricing model can work, three essential positioning questions must be answered:

1. Who is your target customer?
2. What benefits do you bring?
3. How do you solve their problems uniquely better than alternatives?

Positioning must be confirmed and aligned across executive, sales, and marketing leadership before pricing models are developed. Disagreement at the executive level cascades throughout the organization. The 2018 startup case study demonstrates this: a chat-based customer service company experienced internal conflict between the CEO's positioning as "CRM competing with Salesforce" versus the team's "Conversational-AI positioning." This disagreement resulted in mixed sales messaging that changed quarterly, slowing the entire sales engine.

### Introduction to Packaging

Packaging comprises the set of capabilities in an offer—excluding price—that constitutes a bundle of features and services. It standardizes offerings to achieve sales efficiency and maximize return from prospects. Tiered packaging allows firms to capture progressive value across customer segments, with prospects needing more features typically paying more than those needing fewer.

### Two Primary Packaging Approaches

**Good-Better-Best Packaging** works best for large homogenous customer bases with similar willingness to pay. This 2-3 step graded approach targets different customer segments with mapped price points based on their willingness to pay. The Ford Mustang exemplifies this: the Base model EcoBoost Fastback, mid-tier Mustang BULLITT, and high-end Shelby GT500 offer varying horsepower, engine specs, shell, and interiors. Good-Better-Best maximizes revenue from markets with large prospect sets and low variance in willingness to pay, prioritizing deal velocity over fine-tuned pricing.

**Modular Packaging** suits heterogeneous markets with high variance in willingness to pay and average deal size. This flexible approach tailors bespoke offerings to prospects by grouping features into modules based on use cases and customer segment value perception. Modular packaging better captures revenue in smaller markets where flexibility and bespoke-fit packages maximize revenue.

The Salesforce Service Cloud illustrates Good-Better-Best: Essentials package with case management, Professional tier with CRM and case management, and Unlimited tier with 24x7 support and AI features. Amplitude demonstrates tiered packages across a spectrum: Free tier for cost-conscious users, Growth tier for mid-sized companies, and an Enterprise tier for larger organizations.

Pricing strategy depends on market size (n), variance in customer needs, and revenue capture priorities. The decision between approaches can be visualized on a 2x2 matrix considering market size and homogeneity: publish complete pricing for large homogeneous markets to scale sales and maximize value extraction; consider not publishing pricing for limited or heterogeneous markets to give sales teams flexibility.

### Principles for Effective Packaging

Three core principles guide packaging design:

1. **Gradation**: A gradual upward progression of capability across pricing tiers ensures customers perceive differentiation
2. **Package <> Segment Fit**: Each package's capabilities map to a significant representative market segment that is likely to auto-select that tier
3. **No Shelf-ware**: Packages rarely picked by intended segments should be removed; unused features within packages should be eliminated

Amplitude's pricing evolution (2016-2020) illustrates these principles in practice. As the company became a more trusted brand with more features, they evolved their offerings. Importantly, Amplitude does not expose specific pricing for Growth and Enterprise plans to capture greater value from clients with higher price elasticity.

### Modular Packaging Deep Dive

For modular approaches, features must be MECE (Mutually Exclusive Completely Exhaustive) to avoid duplication across modules. Different customer segments value different features; this should drive bundling decisions. Use cases and feature groupings become the basis for pricing modules.

In a fictional CRM example, features A through T map to use cases such as Lead and Opportunity Management, Team Collaboration, and Marketing Database. Both SMB and Enterprise segments suit a base package with Team Collaboration and Lead and Opportunity Management features. Sales Forecasting, Sales Rep Performance Management, and Account Health Reporting become Enterprise-specific add-ons, while Marketing Database serves as an add-on for SMB customers.

Standardized packages either offer more capabilities than needed (shelfware) or lack needed capabilities (leaving money on the table). Modular packaging enables extraction of requisite value from prospects with varying willingness to pay and price elasticity.

### Marketecture and Hidden Differentiators

Marketecture—a marketing-oriented representation of software that breaks a monolithic product into specific modules—uncovers and highlights hidden product differentiators that justify premium pricing. The Medallia case study (2013-2014) illustrates this approach: facing pricing pressure from competitors with similar value propositions, Medallia used a marketecture exercise to package hidden product architecture capabilities as distinct modules, including OrgSync, which denotes capability in mapping complex organizational hierarchies supporting up to 70,000 users. This packaging enabled the sales team to justify premium pricing and put competition on the defensive.

### Decision Framework: Add-ons vs. Main Packages

Features with broad appeal should be integrated into main packages to drive conversion rates. Specialized features catering to niche segments are better suited as add-ons. Too many add-ons can lead to customer confusion and perception of "nickel and diming." Adding features as add-ons is faster and less disruptive than redesigning the entire lineup. The decision should balance popularity, revenue maximization, ease of launch, and customer experience complexity.

### Price Transparency Strategy

Roughly 75% of SaaS companies publish pricing on their websites while 25% do not, according to Chartmogul. The transparency decision should depend on market size and homogeneity, not just following industry norms. High price standardization and transparency can make sales more transactional, while withholding specific pricing allows flexibility but may hurt conversion in large markets.

Companies that innovate and build brand trust can charge more and remove specific pricing from their pages. Free plans make sense only if going after a large TAM (Total Addressable Market) or wanting to undercut competitors—they indicate a company is targeting scale or competitive positioning.

## Definitions and Terms

**Positioning**: How you carve a place of influence in your buyer's mind; how you differentiate your brand in the mind by using an oversimplified message to cut through clutter.

**Heuristic Thinking**: Like muscle memory for the brain that enables micro-second preferences and biases in buyer decisions (Daniel Kahneman's System 1 thinking).

**System 1**: Fast, intuitive thinking based on heuristics and biases (contrasted with System 2 deliberate reasoning).

**Packaging**: The set of capabilities in an offer, excluding price itself, that constitutes a bundle of features and services designed to match feature-sets, services, and price to market segments.

**Good-Better-Best Packaging**: 2-3 step graded packages targeted at different customer segments with mapped price points based on their willingness to pay; prioritizes deal velocity in large homogenous markets.

**Modular Packaging**: A flexible approach to packaging that tailors bespoke offerings to prospects by grouping features into modules based on use cases and customer segment value perception; suits heterogeneous markets.

**Marketecture**: A marketing-oriented hierarchical representation of software that breaks a monolithic product into specific modules with market-oriented names, revealing hidden product differentiators.

**MECE (Mutually Exclusive Completely Exhaustive)**: A principle ensuring features are not duplicated across modules and all key capabilities are covered.

**Willingness to Pay (WTP)**: The price a customer segment is willing to pay for a given package or feature set.

**Price Elasticity**: The responsiveness of customer demand to price changes; differs across industry verticals and market segments.

**First-Mover Advantage**: The competitive edge of being first to establish a position in the buyer's mind; cannot be replicated by competitors without appearing as an emulator.

**Shelfware**: Features or capabilities included in a package that are unused by the target segment; indicates poor package-segment fit.

**Package-Segment Fit**: The alignment between a package's capabilities and a market segment's needs, such that the segment naturally auto-selects that tier.

**Gradation**: A gradual upward progression of capability across pricing tiers.

**Add-ons**: Features or modules priced separately from the main package, typically targeting specialized use cases or niche segments.

**Vendor Persona**: The role and responsibilities of the decision-maker you're targeting (e.g., VP of Sales).

**Buyer Persona**: The profile of your target customer including their priorities, values, and decision-making heuristics.

**Nickel and Diming**: Customer perception that too many separate charges create unfair or excessive pricing.

## Actionable Lessons

### Positioning Phase
1. **Identify buyer priorities and values** to position your product accordingly
2. **Focus on a single differentiating attribute** that resonates with buyer psychology
3. **Establish positioning early** to gain first-mover advantage in the buyer's mind
4. **Use heuristics to understand** how buyers will evaluate your offering relative to known competitors
5. **Research which vendors, peer companies, and thought leaders** your target buyers reference
6. **Answer three essential positioning questions**: Who is your target customer? What benefits do you bring? How do you solve their problems uniquely better than alternatives?
7. **Confirm positioning alignment** with executive team, sales leadership, and marketing leadership across all main market segments before finalizing pricing
8. **Resolve any positioning disagreements** at the executive level before starting a pricing exercise
