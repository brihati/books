# 7. Give a Dog a Good Name

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter teaches Carnegie's principle: **Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.** People often rise toward the reputation others give them. Expecting the best can call it forth. The material frames influence as an exercise in human dignity: people become more receptive when they feel understood, respected, important, and free from humiliation. The chapter's examples and anecdotes are used to turn that principle into behavior a reader can practice in ordinary conversations, sales situations, leadership moments, criticism, disagreement, and requests for cooperation.

## Key Points

- Core principle: Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.
- The practical aim is to reduce defensiveness and increase willing cooperation.
- Carnegie emphasizes the other person's pride, desires, interests, and point of view as the route to influence.
- The method depends on sincerity; mechanical flattery or manipulative technique undercuts the lesson.
- The chapter is best used as a behavioral rule: apply it before trying to persuade, correct, sell, lead, or ask.

## Important Examples and Stories

## Definitions and Terms

- Principle: Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.
- Carnegie method: shift attention from your own demand to the other person's dignity, desire, and point of view.

## Actionable Lessons

- Before acting, ask how the situation looks from the other person's side.
- Use this rule in one concrete sentence or behavior: Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.
- Keep the tone sincere, specific, and respectful.
- Watch for defensiveness; if it appears, return to appreciation, listening, or the other person's interests.
