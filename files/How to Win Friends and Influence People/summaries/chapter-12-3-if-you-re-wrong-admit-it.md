# 3. If You’re Wrong, Admit It

## Detailed Chapter Summary

This chapter teaches Carnegie's principle: **If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.** Prompt admission disarms criticism, preserves credibility, and turns conflict into cooperation. The material frames influence as an exercise in human dignity: people become more receptive when they feel understood, respected, important, and free from humiliation. The chapter's examples and anecdotes are used to turn that principle into behavior a reader can practice in ordinary conversations, sales situations, leadership moments, criticism, disagreement, and requests for cooperation.

## Key Points

- Core principle: If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.
- The practical aim is to reduce defensiveness and increase willing cooperation.
- Carnegie emphasizes the other person's pride, desires, interests, and point of view as the route to influence.
- The method depends on sincerity; mechanical flattery or manipulative technique undercuts the lesson.
- The chapter is best used as a behavioral rule: apply it before trying to persuade, correct, sell, lead, or ask.

## Actionable Lessons

- Before acting, ask how the situation looks from the other person's side.
- Use this rule in one concrete sentence or behavior: If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.
- Keep the tone sincere, specific, and respectful.
- Watch for defensiveness; if it appears, return to appreciation, listening, or the other person's interests.
